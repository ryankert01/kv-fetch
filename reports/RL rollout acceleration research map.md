# Rollouts dominate reasoning RL; hybrids remain unclaimed

Accelerating RL rollouts is an accepted and fast-growing research problem at top venues. The worry that rollout is a minor share of RL time is wrong for the workload that matters in 2026. In synchronous long-chain-of-thought RL, seven independent measurements put generation at **63-91% of step time**, rising above **95% at 32K-token output budgets**. Rollout is a minor cost (about 30-40%) only when outputs are short: a 166-token video task, or 2024-era RLHF with 1K-token responses. In asynchronous pipelines it is hidden behind training, but it still occupies 37-75% of the GPUs. Peer review has caught up. There were about six accepted papers in 2025 and about fifteen at systems venues in 2026, including a dedicated **"RL Training at Scale" session at OSDI'26**, plus a stream of ICLR/ICML papers that co-design algorithm and system. The catch is that the generic levers (long-tail scheduling, partial and async rollouts, speculative decoding, weight sync) are now crowded. Most of that work comes from industry-academic pairs evaluating on 64-1,024 GPUs: **13 of 15 accepted 2026 systems papers have industry co-authors**, and speculative decoding for RL alone has at least 17 papers. What is still open, and matches the student's own measurements, is rollout-side management of the fixed-size recurrent state in hybrid models. Every major hybrid family (Qwen3.5/3.6, Qwen3-Next, Kimi Linear/K3, Nemotron 3, MiniMax-M1) ships RL-trained checkpoints. Yet no paper evaluates hybrid-aware preemption, swap, fork or migration inside an RL loop: the closest work (HARTS, LongStraw) is on the trainer side, and Kimi K3 only offloads the state during partial rollouts. That makes the topic worth choosing, with three conditions. Scope it as an RL-specific state-and-memory system on 2B-35B open hybrids. Judge it by time-to-accuracy, not tokens per second. Aim first at ATC 2027, ICML 2027 or NeurIPS 2027, not at the December 8 OSDI 2027 deadline. MLSys 2027 closes tomorrow (October 30, 2026) and is out of reach.

## Rollout takes 63-91% of a long-reasoning RL step, and the long tail is why

The often-quoted "over 90%" figure is not one of these. OpenRLHF states it in its introduction without a breakdown ([OpenRLHF](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.11143)), and APRIL repeats it by citation ([APRIL](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.18521v3)). The measured numbers are lower, and they are still dominant. Length is the strongest predictor of rollout share. Seer's three workloads rise from 63% to 84% to 87% as average generation grows from 7.6K to 22.4K to 39K tokens, although model and task change with length ([Seer](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.14617)). In RL in the Wild, decode takes **85.4%** of a coding iteration (9.3K average output) but only **11.7%** of a video iteration (166 tokens) ([RL in the Wild](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.25279v1)). Two sweeps are missing from the literature: no paper varies model size at fixed length with numeric shares, and none varies n (samples per prompt) with everything else held fixed.

Rollout becomes a minor share in three regimes. The first is short outputs, as above. The second is asynchronous or disaggregated pipelines, where generation overlaps training. In NeMo-RL's one-step-async configuration, speculative decoding cut exposed generation from **10.4 s to 0.6 s**, yet the step improved only from **75.0 s to 60.5 s (1.24x)**, because async overlap already hid most of the rollout ([NeMo-RL](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.26779)). In these systems the right metric is the share of GPUs devoted to rollout, not the share of step time. Computed from the papers, that share is 50% for LlamaRL at 405B, 37.5% for PipelineRL, 60% for ROLL Flash's best split and 75% for NeMo-RL async ([LlamaRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.24034), [PipelineRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.19128v2), [ROLL Flash](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.11345)). Faster rollout there cuts GPU cost or staleness rather than wall-clock time. The third regime is very large n with down-sampling: PODS argues rollout is "embarrassingly parallel and memory-light" and that the policy update becomes the cost ([PODS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.13818)). Agentic RL also leans async because environment latency dominates, which is why GLM-4.5 uses colocated synchronous RL for reasoning but disaggregated async RL for SWE agents ([GLM-4.5](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.06471)).

Amdahl's law limits what rollout acceleration buys. In synchronous NeMo-RL, generation that was **1.54x and 1.77x faster** gave steps that were **1.35x and 1.41x faster**. That matches the formula 1/((1−f)+f/s), which predicts 1.40x for f=0.661 and s=1.77 ([NeMo-RL](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.26779)). The 10-35% that remains (logprob recompute plus training) caps any rollout-only gain at about 3-10x. The distribution of time inside rollout matters more than its average throughput. Seer reports that in memory-constrained tasks the last 10% of requests take up to **50% of rollout time**. In one Qwen2-VL-72B iteration on veRL it counted **13,686 preemptions**, and the last 5% of requests did not even start until 42% of the iteration had passed ([Seer](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.14617)). RLHFuse finds that fewer than 1% of samples take more than 50% of generation time ([RLHFuse](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.13221)). DynaRL finds that long-tail idling can waste up to 60% of compute ([DynaRL](https://www.usenix.org/conference/osdi26/presentation/wang-yuanqing)). So a technique that shortens the tail or relieves memory pressure is worth more than one that raises peak tokens per second. A secondary blog puts rollout at roughly 20% of FLOPs but a median 68.5% of time ([zenn.dev](https://zenn.dev/tosshi/articles/99375dd5ac013f?locale=en)); this is unverified against primary data, but it is consistent with decode being memory-bound. The dollar stakes are large. TLT's 32B run took **11 days for 385 steps on 128 GPUs** ([TLT](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.16665)). DeepSeek-R1-Zero's RL used about **101K H800-hours** ([DeepSeek-R1 supplement](https://media.springernature.com/original/springer-static/esm/art%3A10.1038%2Fs41586-025-09422-z/MediaObjects/41586_2025_9422_MOESM1_ESM.pdf)). MiniMax-M1's RL cost **$534.7K** in rented H800 time ([MiniMax-M1](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.13585)).

### Long-tail mitigation splits into relaxing, reordering and shrinking

Long-tail work falls into three families. The first relaxes synchrony. Kimi K1.5 gives each rollout a token budget and carries unfinished trajectories into later iterations, but reports no speedup number ([Kimi K1.5](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.12599)). APRIL over-provisions requests twofold and carries unfinished ones forward. It reports **+22.5% average throughput** on a single 8-GPU node, with about 40% of later-step tokens coming from carried-over trajectories and up to five policy versions mixed in one trajectory, all without importance-sampling correction ([APRIL](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.18521v3)). CoPRIS and UloRL report 1.94x and 2.06x ([CoPRIS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.05589), [UloRL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.19766)). The second family stays exactly on-policy and reorders work. RollPacker defers long prompts to dedicated "long rounds" ([RollPacker](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.21009)). Seer uses the earliest-finishing sibling in a GRPO group to estimate the group's length and reaches **96% of oracle throughput** ([Seer](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.14617v3)). TailSieve routes predicted-long prompts to a low-concurrency pool: up to 1.67x from routing alone, and 2.59x with speculative decoding ([TailSieve](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.22788)). The third family shrinks the tail itself: DARTS selects concise trajectories ([DARTS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.30859)), and GRESO skips prompts that will not yield a learning signal ([GRESO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.02177)). The finding that matters most for this student is that over-provisioning collides with memory. Seer beat Partial Rollout by **43%** because partial rollout's doubled request volume made preemption worse ([Seer](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.14617v3)). RollPacker's over-provisioned short rounds had **1.79x more preemptions** than its long rounds ([RollPacker](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.21009v1)).

### Colocated synchronous for reasoning, disaggregated asynchronous for agents

Colocated systems put rollout and training on the same GPUs, so rollout sits on the critical path. Examples are verl, Kimi K1.5/K2, DeepSeek-R1, Seer and slime's synchronous mode. Their engineering burden is memory handoff. Kimi kills and restarts vLLM because CUDA graphs and NCCL buffers make full memory release hard ([Kimi K1.5](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.12599)). verl recommends giving rollout only **0.5-0.7 of GPU memory** ([verl tuning](https://verl.readthedocs.io/en/latest/perf/perf_tuning.html)), well below vLLM's 0.9 serving default. Disaggregated systems split the roles. StreamRL argues that generation is memory-bandwidth-bound while training is compute-bound, runs generation on H20s and training on H800s, and reports 1.30-2.66x ([StreamRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.15930)). Asynchronous systems hide rollout behind training at the cost of bounded staleness. AReaL bounds staleness at η of 4 to 8 ([AReaL](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.24298)), and Laminar removes the global weight-sync barrier entirely ([Laminar](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.12633v1)). LlamaRL reports 10.7x at 405B, mostly from giving generator and trainer different parallelism ([LlamaRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.24034)). PipelineRL and Magistral swap weights mid-generation without recomputing the KV cache ([PipelineRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.19128v2), [Magistral](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.10910)). The async speedups of 1.5-5.5x mostly measure how much tail idle the synchronous baseline wastes. On-policy accelerations (Seer, RollPacker, TLT, RhymeRL) recover 1.4-2.6x without the staleness trade-off. That is why "fast synchronous RL" became a headline contribution in 2026.

### Speculative decoding pays only once the batch drains

Speculative decoding (SD) is the most crowded sub-area, with at least 17 papers between August 2025 and October 2026. The drafters fall into four kinds. **Model-free history drafters** reuse earlier rollouts (RhymeRL, DAS, SRT, NgramRL, Seer's grouped suffix trees, WAR, BubbleSpec). **Learned drafters** are adapted online (TLT, ReSpec, FastGRPO, GrowMTP, Bebop). **Self-speculation** derives the drafter from the policy itself (EfficientRollout). **Deliberately lossy reuse** relaxes acceptance: SPEC-RL with lenience ℓ>1 reaches 1.94-2.88x rollout speedup but gives no bias bound ([SPEC-RL](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.23232v3)). Nearly every paper switches SD on only at low concurrency. TLT waits until fewer than 32 requests remain ([TLT](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.16665v3)); RhymeRL and EfficientRollout use profiled thresholds ([RhymeRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.18588v1), [EfficientRollout](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.18967)). In RL, then, SD is effectively a long-tail technique. RhymeRL finds that **75-95% of tokens** are reusable across epochs, with 65-79% acceptance, and says concurrently trained draft models "often failed" ([RhymeRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.18588v1)). ReSpec names the three standing problems: diminishing gains at large batch, drafter staleness, and drafter-induced policy degradation ([ReSpec](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.26475)). Exactness comes in three tiers. Standard rejection sampling is exact (TLT, NeMo-RL, Bebop, BubbleSpec). Multi-path tree drafts are claimed lossless, but the papers rarely spell out how verification works under high-temperature sampling (Seer, DAS). And some reuse is openly lossy (SPEC-RL).

### Migration mostly re-prefills instead of moving KV

Most systems migrate a request by moving its tokens and rebuilding the KV cache with a prefill. RhymeRL found migration overhead exceeded recomputation and migrates 2.2-5.5% of math samples ([RhymeRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.18588v1)). RollPacker moves GPUs between roles in under 3 s by recompute ([RollPacker](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.21009v1)). RLBoost re-prefills partial responses after spot preemption, which cuts overhead by 33-76% compared with regenerating from scratch ([RLBoost](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.19225v3)). Seer is the exception. It ships KV through a Mooncake-based DRAM/SSD pool, because chunk-level rescheduling would otherwise force full recompute and one iteration produces "tens of terabytes of KVCache" ([Seer](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.14617v3)). Laminar's 0.69 s repack does not say whether it moves KV ([Laminar](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.12633v1)). No paper sweeps the crossover between moving and recomputing across context length, model size, interconnect and architecture.

### Sharing the prompt across n samples saves little in math RL

Engines already share a group's prompt through copy-on-write. The only rigorous measurement is from serving, not RL: parallel sampling saves **6.1-9.8%** of KV memory on Alpaca and 16.2-30.5% on ShareGPT. vLLM also gang-schedules and gang-preempts the sequences of a group ([vLLM SOSP'23](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.06180)). In math RLVR, prompts average **126.5 input tokens against 9,839 output tokens**, so sharing them saves almost nothing ([RL in the Wild](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.25279v1)). The 2025-26 prefix-sharing papers therefore target the trainer. DualKV speeds the policy update 1.63-2.09x ([DualKV](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.15422)). PrefixFlow gains 1.90x on long-prefix RAG but notes "suffix-dominated splits gain little" ([PrefixFlow](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.01143)). Prefix caching across weight updates is also a correctness hazard: ServiceNow turned it off in PipelineRL's vLLM V1 port ([ServiceNow](https://hface.pages.dev/blog/ServiceNow-AI/correctness-before-corrections)). Rollout-side sharing pays off in multimodal and multi-turn agentic RL. Tool-use inputs reach a p90 of 2,802 tokens ([RL in the Wild](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.25279v1)), and MISA-T's residency-aware KV accounting beats a tuned cache-aware router by 43.6-53.3% ([MISA-T](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.11152)).

### Weight sync fell from a third of the step to a few percent

In 2024 weight sync was a bottleneck. OpenRLHF needed **111.65 s** for 70B ([LlamaRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.24034)), and an OpenRLHF figure put sync at up to 36.4% of iteration time when trainer and generator sit on different devices ([HybridFlow](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.19256)). By 2026 it was engineered down to **2-6%** of the iteration in Seer and about 1-2% in NeMo-RL ([Seer](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.14617), [NeMo-RL](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.26779)). For a 1T model, Perplexity reports ~1.3 s with one-sided RDMA ([Perplexity](https://research.perplexity.ai/articles/weight-transfer-for-rl-post-training-in-under-2-seconds)), Kimi K2 under 30 s ([Kimi K2](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.20534)), and SGLang's Mooncake P2P path cut Kimi-K2 from 53.3 s to 7.2 s ([LMSYS](https://lmsys.org/blog/2026-04-29-p2p-update/)). The topic stays live for elastic, cross-datacenter and serving-GPU rollouts (ROSE, TensorHub), but it is no longer the main lever.

### Low-precision rollouts trade 1.2-2x speed for a mismatch problem

FP8, INT8, NVFP4 and INT4-KV rollouts buy roughly 1.2-2x. FP8-RL reports up to 44% higher rollout throughput ([FP8-RL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.18150)) and QuRL 20-80% ([QuRL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.13953)). The cost is that rollout and trainer disagree on token probabilities even with identical weights ([Feng Yao et al.](https://fengyao.notion.site/off-policy-rl); presented at the [OPT 2025 NeurIPS workshop](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2025/124259), not the main track). Jet-RL reports "catastrophic accuracy collapse" for BF16-train/FP8-rollout on long horizons ([Jet-RL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.14243)), and INT4 KV degrades learning even with token-level TIS ([KV4-RL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.14448)). vLLM's October 2026 bitwise-consistency work keeps **91.3% of rollout throughput** and argues that TIS, MIS and router replay "reduce the effect of the mismatch but do not remove it" ([vLLM blog](https://vllm.ai/blog/2026-10-14-train-inference-consistency)).

## The papers' own limitations converge on staleness, memory and measurement

The future-work statements cluster into six themes.

**Staleness is not characterized.** AReaL finds unbounded staleness underperforms even with its decoupled objective ([AReaL](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.24298v5)). APRIL calls the effect of mixing many earlier policies an "open question" ([APRIL](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.18521v3)). A September 2026 survey of 86 rollout-efficiency works finds that most systems report a single staleness bound, so the trade-off between staleness and model quality has not been mapped ([survey](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.25463)). Newer papers are already repairing the side effects of partial rollouts: RollVerify truncates invalid stale suffixes ([RollVerify](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.09914)), and another paper shows that async pipelines lose the "old logits" that decoupled corrections need ([arXiv 2605.12070](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.12070)).

**Drafters go stale and start cold.** Drafters drift as the policy changes ([EfficientRollout](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.18967)). Pre-RL draft training costs extra compute ([GrowMTP](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.16648)). TLT's drafter training is limited by the size of the idle window ([TLT](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.16665v3)). History-based methods give nothing in epoch 1 ([SPEC-RL](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.23232v3), [RhymeRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.18588v1)).

**The scope is narrow.** Evaluations are almost all single-turn math and code. AReaL, TLT and SPEC-RL explicitly leave multi-turn agentic RL to future work. RollPacker's planner covers TP but not expert parallelism, and Seer's adaptation to async RL is untested ([RollPacker](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.21009v1), [Seer](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.14617v3)).

**Key policies are heuristics.** AReaL's 75/25 split of GPUs between inference and training is a fixed guess. DAS's latency model has about 12% error ([DAS](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.13841v1)). The survey notes that length predictors have never been compared on a common workload ([survey](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.25463)).

**Mismatch corrections reduce the gap but do not remove it.** That is the vLLM blog's verdict above. An MXFP8 study adds that 5 of 7 weight-sync failure conditions failed silently ([arXiv 2610.34915](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.34915)).

**Measurement is inconsistent.** Only **12 of 80** surveyed methods report gains on both the system and the algorithm side, which the authors call a principal source of incomparability. They propose accelerator-hours-to-target-quality as the common metric ([survey](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.25463)). Laminar had to reimplement closed baselines, and its scaling efficiency stays below 60% ([Laminar](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.12633v1)).

Beyond what the authors admit, the notes found holes nobody claims. No paper compares swap, recompute and KV-pool fetch for RL rollouts at 16K-128K tokens. Only Seer and RollPacker report preemption counts at all ([Seer](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.14617v3), [RollPacker](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.21009v1)). No RL paper measures rollout-side KV saved by sharing a GRPO group's prompt. The balance between over-provisioning and KV pressure is unexplored, despite Seer's and RollPacker's evidence that it causes preemption storms. Rollouts have a structural asymmetry: the decoding batch shrinks through the tail, so memory pressure peaks early in a step and compute idles late. Seer's global pool is the only design that exploits it. Hybrid models do not appear in any of these papers' evaluations.

## Hybrid models break three assumptions rollout systems were built on

Every system in the previous sections assumes a dense-attention policy. Per-request memory grows token by token in small pages. Prefix sharing is free at block granularity. Swapping loses to recompute because transfers are small. Hybrid models with linear attention, Mamba or GDN layers break all three assumptions. These models are now standard RL targets.

### Every hybrid family ships RL checkpoints, and every report fights mismatch

MiniMax-M1, a lightning-attention hybrid, traced unstable RL to training-inference probability mismatch. Raising the LM head to FP32 moved the correlation "from approximately 0.9x to 0.99x" ([MiniMax-M1](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.13585v1)). MiniMax-M2 then returned to full attention. Its stated reasons were that linear attention is "far more sensitive to numerical precision," that its infrastructure is "much less mature," and an open question about how to do SD on a linear-attention backbone ([MiniMax blog](https://huggingface.co/blog/MiniMax-AI/why-did-m2-end-up-as-a-full-attention-model)). The rest of the field moved the other way.

Qwen3.5 describes "a scalable asynchronous RL framework" with "fully disaggregated training-inference architecture", FP8 end-to-end, rollout router replay, speculative decoding and "multi-turn rollout locking." It claims 3-5x, gives no GPU counts and says nothing specific to GDN ([Qwen3.5 blog](https://www.alibabacloud.com/blog/qwen3-5-towards-native-multimodal-agents_602894)). Kimi Linear added TIS for the same precision problem ([Kimi Linear](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.26692v2)). Ring-linear found that a BF16 linear-attention state makes "errors accumulate progressively", and saw the same token at probability 0 in training and 1 in inference ([Ring-linear](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.19338)). Nemotron 3 Nano ran synchronous GRPO with 128 prompts × 16 generations at 49K and masked IS ([Nemotron 3 Nano](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.20848)). Nemotron 3 Super moved to one-step-async with in-flight weight updates and explicitly does "not recompute the KV cache after updating the model weights" ([Nemotron 3 Super](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.12374v1)). Falcon-H1R found that G=32 "substantially increased generation time" without gains ([Falcon-H1R](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.02346v1)).

Only Kimi K3 (69 KDA + 24 MLA layers) treats the recurrent state as a first-class object in rollout infrastructure. During partial rollouts, "KDA states are offloaded and prefetched together with the corresponding MLA KV cache blocks" in a CPU-DRAM pool. That pool is **released after every rollout iteration**, so no state survives a policy update ([Kimi K3](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.24653v1)). No hybrid report gives a rollout-time breakdown attributable to the recurrent layers.

### A fixed 63-147 MiB state makes fork, swap and migration cheap in principle

A hybrid request carries a recurrent state of fixed size plus a smaller attention KV that grows with length. vLLM's blog gives about 2.57 MiB per sequence for Nemotron-Nano-12B-v2 and notes that at 128K "the KV cache can be nearly 200 times larger than the Mamba state." vLLM inflates the attention block size to match the state page, for example to 672 tokens ([vLLM hybrid blog](https://pytorch.org/blog/hybrid-models-as-first-class-citizens-in-vllm/)). Kimi K3's KDA state blocks are about 54 MB against roughly 27 KB MLA blocks, and "KDA state cannot be sharded by token position" ([LMSYS K3](https://lmsys.org/blog/2026-07-27-kimi-k3-day0-support/)).

The student's measurements on vLLM 0.32 fit this picture and sharpen it: **63 MiB per request for Qwen3.6-35B-A3B-FP8, about 147 MiB for Qwen3.5-27B-FP8**, with block sizes of 528-1056 tokens. Two consequences follow. The first is my estimate from vLLM's page-size rule. If one attention block page equals one GDN layer's state, a 3:1 GDN:attention model's full recurrent state equals the attention KV of about 3 × block-size tokens, i.e. 1.6K-3.2K tokens. That state is the majority of a request's footprint until then, and still 9-16% at 16K. The second is computed directly. At concurrency 64, the 27B model's recurrent states alone take **9.2 GiB**. One GRPO group of n=16 takes 2.3 GiB before it decodes a single token. That arrives at once, in large blocks, which is a different memory shape from PagedAttention's 16-token pages.

This changes the old trade-offs. vLLM V1 defaults to recompute rather than swap ([vLLM docs](https://docs.vllm.ai/en/latest/configuration/optimization.html)). The SOSP'23 argument behind that default was that small blocks mean many small PCIe transfers, which made recompute never more than 20% of swap latency ([vLLM SOSP'23](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.06180)). A contiguous multi-MiB state per layer is the opposite case. Meanwhile, re-prefilling a hybrid request still runs every recurrent layer over the full context. So swap and migrate should beat recompute far more often for hybrids. That is an inference no paper has measured. Forking is the cheapest case: one prefill, then n state copies plus shared attention KV.

The existing work stops short of the rollout loop. **HARTS** exports chunk-boundary states for agentic rollout trees and reports 4.77-10.45x higher update throughput, but it is a trainer-side system on AReaL and Megatron ([HARTS](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.28158)). **LongStraw** captures a GRPO group's prompt state once, also on the trainer ([LongStraw](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.14952)). **SGLang** copies state on a radix hit and transfers Mamba state atomically in prefill-decode disaggregation ([SGLang hybrid blog](https://pytorch.org/blog/hybrid-models-meet-sglang-more-than-full-attention/)), and **Kimi K3** offloads state during partial rollouts. No paper evaluates hybrid-aware preemption, swap, migration or cross-iteration state persistence inside an RL loop.

The student's profiling supplies the missing symptom. Under memory pressure, hybrids show readmission ping-pong: at concurrency 64 the MoE hybrid hit **645 preemptions against 346** for the dense comparator, and the 27B hit **1,006**, cut to 46 by `--watermark 0.1` for a 28% gain. RL rollouts make this worse than serving. A synchronous GRPO step releases all P×n requests together. Their lengths grow toward 16-32K. In colocated setups they run at verl's 0.5-0.7 memory fraction. Seer's 13,686 preemptions were measured on a non-hybrid model; nobody has published the hybrid equivalent.

### Prefix caching cannot share recurrent state inside a GRPO step

Hybrid prefix caching works only at coarse checkpoints. Marconi (MLSys'25) frames why: in-place state updates make reuse all-or-nothing ([Marconi](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.19379v3)). vLLM's align mode stores state only at block-aligned step ends. Its RFC #45702 states that vLLM "does not currently support reusing a Mamba state that is produced by another request in the same scheduler step" and that equal block sizes force roughly 1,024-token granularity ([vLLM RFC #45702](https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/issues/45702)). This is exactly the GRPO pattern: n siblings arrive in the same step, so the recurrent prefix is computed once per sample unless the engine forks state explicitly. vLLM PR #61192 (merged October 19, 2026) works around it with a warm-up request. That cut 16 questions on a Qwen3.5-architecture 27B from **106.5 s to 12.0 s** and lifted the hit rate from 0.37 to 0.94, but only for one endpoint and not for general n>1 sampling ([vLLM PR #61192](https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/pull/61192)).

Multi-turn agentic RL suits hybrid caching better because reuse points fall on turn boundaries. Kimi K3 checkpoints there ([Kimi K3](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.24653v1)), and Fireworks reports **~99% hit rates** on K3 multi-turn rollouts with cache "scoped to the correct adapter and policy version" ([Fireworks](https://fireworks.ai/blog/kimi-k3-on-fireworks-fast-rl-at-frontier-scale)). Every weight update invalidates cached states semantically, so systems either salt the cache per policy version (TorchTitan RL ([Wang blog](https://yichuan-w.github.io/blog/GDN-train-inference-mismatch-asyncRL/)), Fireworks) or knowingly reuse stale state (Nemotron 3 Super). No one has studied a policy for caching hybrid state across iterations.

### Speculation gains shrink and exact parity costs 20-40% throughput

Speculative decoding on hybrids needs state rollback. That is now engineered through per-draft state slots in SGLang, STree ([NeurIPS'25](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/hash/a7efd7734bb2e79997f8d1137ece3f09-Abstract-Conference.html)), ReplaySSM's input-replay buffers at 1.87-1.96x ([ReplaySSM](https://tridao.me/blog/2026/replayssm/)), and KVBuffer ([KVBuffer](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.19049)). Yet the one RL rollout study with hybrid targets finds smaller gains. Qwen3.5-35B-A3B with DFlash reached an acceptance length of 4.58 but only **1.50x rollout / 1.46x end-to-end**. Nemotron-3.5-Lightning reached 1.19x. Dense Qwen3-8B reached up to 2.23x. The authors list linear-attention targets as future work ([arXiv 2609.07108](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.07108)). Engine bugs here quietly corrupt the data. vLLM #62304 had the GDN state miss the last accepted token's update under n-gram drafting in v0.31.0, which was clean in v0.32.0 ([vLLM #62304](https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/issues/62304)). A bug like that changes the sampled distribution with no visible error.

The mismatch problem is structural. Training uses chunkwise-parallel GDN kernels; rollout uses chunked prefill and a token-by-token recurrent decode, so the two sides run different algorithms. Bitwise parity has been shown, but at a price. On Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, sampling fell from **7,863 to 4,767 tok/s (−39.4%)** and the RL step slowed from 374.3 s to 447.0 s ([Panda](https://kiddyboots216.github.io/mismatch/)). Full batch invariance in TorchTitan RL cost 2-3x on MATH and about 5x on a 64-turn agent, with mixed accuracy benefit ([Wang](https://yichuan-w.github.io/blog/GDN-train-inference-mismatch-asyncRL/)). Megatron's in-repo engine reached 0 of 486,586 tokens mismatched on Nemotron-3-Nano ([Megatron PR #8646](https://github.com/NVIDIA/Megatron-LM/pull/8646)). State precision matters a great deal. A silent fallback to a BF16 SSM cache made **23% of generations hit the token cap, against 0.25% with FP32**, and dropped GPQA-D from 68.4 to 51.9 ([vLLM PR #63218](https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/pull/63218)). On Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, a prompt's logprobs shift by up to 0.198 depending on which other sequences share the engine step ([vLLM #59764](https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/issues/59764)). Batch composition changes continuously as a rollout drains through its tail, so this batch dependence matters for RL.

## The top of the field is industrial and the middle is thin

Counted by venue, the topic went from roughly zero verified top-venue papers in 2024 to about six in 2025, and about fifteen at systems venues in 2026 plus six or seven at ICLR/ICML. OSDI'26 gave it a named session, while none of SOSP'25's 66 accepted papers was about RL ([OSDI'26 program](https://www.usenix.org/conference/osdi26/technical-sessions), [SOSP'25 list](https://sigops.org/s/conferences/sosp/2025/accepted.html)). The preprint flow is larger. The September 2026 survey kept **86 works** from January 2024 to September 2026 ([survey](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.25463)), and one narrow arXiv query returned 72 CS papers in 2026 alone ([arXiv search](https://arxiv.org/search/advanced?advanced=&terms-0-operator=AND&terms-0-term=rollout&terms-0-field=abstract&terms-1-operator=AND&terms-1-term=%22reinforcement+learning%22&terms-1-field=abstract&terms-2-operator=AND&terms-2-term=throughput&terms-2-field=abstract&classification-computer_science=y&classification-physics_archives=all&date-filter_by=specific_year&date-year=2026&date-date_type=submitted_date&abstracts=hide&size=50&order=-announced_date_first)). The topic is thesis-shaped: an HKUST PhD qualifying exam in April 2026 names "the rollout bottleneck" as one of three system challenges in RL post-training ([HKUST](https://cse.hkust.edu.hk/pg/defenses/S26/lcaoar-15-04-2026.html)).

The accepted work is concentrated. Of the 15 systems-venue papers, about 9 have a large-lab co-author (ByteDance, Alibaba, Moonshot, NVIDIA, Amazon), about 4 come from AI-infrastructure firms, and only about 2 are mostly academic. One HKUST group with Alibaba holds 3 of the 15. Many popular frameworks never went to a venue (OpenRLHF, ROLL, slime, StreamRL, LlamaRL). So arXiv volume overstates competition at venues, but reviewers know these systems, which raises the novelty bar.

### Accepted systems papers run on 64-1,024 GPUs

Evaluation scale at systems venues runs from **64 H100s (TLT)** through 128 H800s (RollPacker) and 256 H800s (Seer) to **1,024 H800s (Laminar)**. The smallest MLSys'26 example, DAS, used a single 8×H100 node ([DAS](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.13841v1)), and ReSpec used 3B-14B models ([ReSpec](https://mlsys.org/virtual/2026/poster/3613)). The ML venues accept smaller setups when correctness is part of the claim. Async RLHF used 1B-8B models ([arXiv 2410.18252](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.18252v3)), GRESO used 1.5B-32B ([GRESO](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/hash/b42fb82fc3ffb88b872c4714f093875d-Abstract-Conference.html)), and DistillSparse reports its rollout speedup on a single H200 ([DistillSparse](https://iclr.cc/virtual/2026/10013424)). The notes found no accepted paper in this area evaluated on one GPU. Reviewers now expect four things. Accuracy preservation must be shown: TLT says "preserving model accuracy" and DAS reports "identical training curves". The baseline should be veRL at 16K-32K output lengths. Results should be reported as time-to-quality, not rollout throughput alone. And the paper needs a mechanism, not another scheduler heuristic or another "SD for RL." Trace-driven evaluation has precedent (TLT's self-collected traces, RhymeRL's 64-GPU traces) and is the natural way to project single-node results to scale.

### Four routes fit a lab with a few 48 GB cards

The student's six shared 48 GB cards are roughly a slow, PCIe-connected version of the single 8-GPU node behind DAS (MLSys'26), APRIL and SPEC-RL. That supports four routes.

**ML venues (ICML, NeurIPS).** Algorithm-system co-design on 1.5B-9B hybrids with time-to-accuracy curves, where correctness under staleness, state precision or caching is the contribution. EfficientRollout reached an ICML'26 page with only a 13-16% end-to-end gain, because it brought a new mechanism (a quantized self-drafter); BubbleSpec got in with 1.8x rollout throughput.

**MLSys or ATC.** A per-device memory or state-management mechanism evaluated on one node, with traces for scale. PrefixFlow, a single-mechanism paper, lists ATC'26 acceptance in its arXiv comment.

**Workshops.** ICML/NeurIPS efficiency and RL workshops, or EuroMLSys, for an early version.

**An industry partner or a cluster-time grant**, which is effectively required for OSDI, NSDI or EuroSys, given that 13 of 15 accepted systems papers had industry co-authors.

Small upstream fixes to vLLM, such as the ping-pong report, build credibility on any route. They are not a paper by themselves.

## Six niches ranked for a student with six shared 48 GB GPUs

The ranking multiplies openness by feasibility. Openness means no published RL-loop evaluation and no upstream patch already absorbing the idea. Feasibility means a first result on one node, with 2B-35B models, within about three months. Every candidate must exploit structure that serving lacks: a known group size n, synchronized release of all P×n requests, cross-epoch length history, weight-version boundaries, and a trainer that consumes the states. That structure is the moat. The student's day-2 profiling found an upstream vLLM PR for every serving-side cost it measured, so a generic serving fix will be absorbed before it becomes a paper.

| Rank | Niche | Openness | Feasibility | Closest prior work |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | State-aware admission, preemption and swap for synchronous hybrid GRPO rollouts | High | High | Seer KV pool; RollPacker preemption-driven TP; Laminar repack; MISA-T; Kimi K3 KDA offload |
| 2 | Version-aware recurrent state for partial and async rollouts (keep, replay or recompute) | High | Medium | Kimi K3 (clears pool); Nemotron 3 Super (keeps stale cache); AReaL (recomputes); RollVerify |
| 3 | Rollout-side fork for GRPO groups and agentic branches, with fork-point states handed to the trainer | Medium | High | vLLM #61192/#45702; SGLang MambaRadixCache; Marconi; HARTS; LongStraw |
| 4 | Migrate-or-recompute crossover for hybrid long-tail rebalancing | High | Medium-low | RhymeRL; RLBoost; RLHFuse; Seer |
| 5 | Cheap train-rollout parity for chunked vs recurrent GDN kernels | Medium | Medium | Panda/XoRL; TorchTitan RL; Megatron #8646; vLLM consistency work |
| 6 | Speculative decoding for hybrid rollouts | Low | High | arXiv 2609.07108; ReplaySSM; STree; KVBuffer; Bebop; GrowMTP |

**Rank 1** is closest to the student's evidence. Its ingredients are the measured ping-pong (645 vs 346 preemptions; 1,006 cut to 46 by the watermark), the 63-147 MiB state, and memory pressure that RL makes worse. The mechanism would use RL's length predictability, which serving lacks: RhymeRL sees only 2-4% of responses change rank across epochs ([RhymeRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.18588v1)), and Seer's group-based estimate reaches 96% of oracle ([Seer](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.14617v3)). With those predictions, a policy can admit a group only when its predicted peak of state plus KV fits, pick preemption victims by predicted remaining length, and swap the contiguous state plus attention KV to host instead of recomputing it. The ceiling is modest but publishable. If such a policy raised rollout throughput 1.3x at a rollout share of 0.70-0.85, Amdahl gives **1.19-1.24x per step**. That is comparable to APRIL's +22.5% and EfficientRollout's 13-16% end-to-end gain. The main risk is that a tuned watermark already removes most of the waste. The one-day kill test is to replay a real GRPO rollout trace through vLLM 0.32 (P×n requests released together, 16K cap, 0.5-0.7 memory fraction) with the best watermark, and compare makespan against a no-preemption oracle. Drop the niche if the headroom is under 15%.

**Rank 2** is the most open intellectually and fits ICML or NeurIPS 2027 as a co-design paper. Kimi K3 discards states every iteration, Nemotron 3 Super keeps stale caches, and AReaL recomputes. Nobody has measured whether stale *recurrent* state hurts more than stale attention KV. There is a reason to expect a difference: in DeltaNet "the state error … participates in the future update dynamics" ([Zhang et al.](https://yifanzhang-pro.github.io/Pretraining-RL-Science/)). APRIL's roughly 40% carried-over tokens show how much re-prefill keeping state could save. Feasibility is medium, because it needs real GRPO runs, though 2B-4B Qwen3.5 runs fit four to six 48 GB cards. A null result would turn the niche into an engineering feature. The kill test is to update weights one step mid-trajectory and measure continuation KL over about 1K tokens with kept versus recomputed state.

**Rank 3** fixes a real engine limitation, but its payoff depends on the workload. Math prompts average 126 tokens, so duplicated prefill across a group is negligible there. The gain sits in agentic, multimodal and long-prompt RL. The student's earlier K0 study found only 2.6-5.5% headroom on Mooncake serving traces, and flagged long-output tool loops of thinking models as the untested case, which is exactly agentic RL. Upstream is moving (PR #61192, RFC #39647 ([vLLM](https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/issues/39647))), and HARTS owns the trainer side. That argues for folding fork into the Rank 1 system rather than publishing it alone. The kill test is to measure duplicated recurrent prefill per step on an agentic RL trace, and drop it below 10% of rollout time.

**Rank 4** is open but scale-bound. The case for it is my inference: for a 3:1 hybrid, the bytes moved per context token are about a quarter of a same-depth dense model's, while re-prefill still runs every layer. That should push RhymeRL's dense-model verdict, that migration costs more than recompute, toward moving state. But rebalancing pays off across 64+ engines, and on PCIe cards it needs simulation. It is best as a section of the Rank 1 system.

**Rank 5** has clear demand: parity costs 20-40% of sampling throughput, or 2-5x with full batch invariance, and its accuracy benefit is unproven. But NVIDIA, vLLM, TorchTitan and XoRL are all on it in October 2026.

**Rank 6** should be avoided as a lead topic. Spec decoding for RL is saturated, hybrid gains are smaller by construction, and the student's own profiling found hybrid MTP already beating dense EAGLE3 with no GDN verification bottleneck.

Also avoid generic long-tail schedulers, async frameworks and dense-model weight sync. Those areas are crowded and their results depend on scale.

## Conclusion

The "minor share" worry turns out to be a question about two dials, output length and synchrony, and both point toward rollout mattering more over time. RL output budgets keep growing: Nemotron 3.5 trains to 128K ([Nemotron 3.5](https://research.nvidia.com/labs/nemotron/files/NVIDIA-Nemotron-3.5-Super-and-Lightning-Technical-Report.pdf)), MiniMax-M1 to 80K ([MiniMax-M1](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.13585)), Kimi K1.5 to a 128K context ([Kimi K1.5](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.12599)). And asynchrony does not remove rollout cost; it turns rollout time into rollout GPUs. The real bottleneck inside rollout is memory and the tail, not FLOPs. Hybrid models change the unit of memory from a token-granular page to a large, fixed, per-request block. That quietly invalidates three heuristics rollout systems inherited from PagedAttention: recompute beats swap, groups are gang-preempted, and prefixes are shared at block granularity.

A PhD topic here is worth choosing if it treats the recurrent state's lifecycle in RL as the object of study. That lifecycle runs from admission, through preemption and swap, fork and persistence across policy versions, to handoff to the trainer. The topic should exploit RL's structure: known n, synchronized arrival, predictable lengths and version boundaries. Engine maintainers will not build those for serving, and large labs have so far shipped them only as undocumented pieces. The sequence that fits the hardware and the calendar has three steps. First, run the Rank 1 kill test on real GRPO traces this month. Second, target a mechanism paper at ATC or ICML 2027 in January. Third, carry the stale-state study (Rank 2) to NeurIPS 2027, and keep the full multi-GPU system for a 2028 systems venue with an industry partner.
