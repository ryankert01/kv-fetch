Fast decoding has a length tax

Most methods that make a language model faster change a few of the tokens it writes. A reasoning model can react to those tokens by writing a longer chain of thought. This page calls the extra tokens the length tax. In some cases the tax is larger than the time the method saved. The page introduces that problem and three other questions from the search.

How the questions were found. AI agents (Claude) ran a literature search in one day. Search agents read papers in nine areas and proposed 40 questions. For the best nine, checking agents looked for earlier answers and tried to predict the result the way an expert would; a question that is easy to predict scored low. A final critic agent looked for what the others had missed. “The checks” on this page means these agents, not people.

How to read the claims. Every search for earlier answers was cut short by rate limits at arXiv and Semantic Scholar, so “open” and “not shown” mean only “the search did not find it”. None of the four questions survived its check as first written; the page shows the narrower versions. The numbers come from papers and from the kv-fetch and SubSpec experiment logs, as read by the agents. A second set of agents re-checked every number on this page, and this version includes their corrections. No person has re-read the papers. Drawings marked Sketch show an idea and contain no data.

Background in one picture

Speculative decoding has two parts. A cheap draft proposes several tokens. The full model then checks all of them in one pass. The methods differ in how strict that check is.

The same trade appears outside speculative decoding. Quantizing weights, quantizing or evicting the KV cache, and sparse attention all act like the bottom row: the model runs faster per token and nothing checks the tokens.

Question 1 · the strongest of the four

Why do lossy speedups make reasoning models think longer?

What has been measured

A relaxed check keeps more tokens per round. In one study the completions also grew. Averaged over all model pairs, they grew by more than the check gained. For the pairs whose target is a reasoning model, the growth was smaller than the gain.

So a relaxed check is usually not a loss. In the median cell it still needs 7% fewer rounds than exact checking, a small net win. In about a third of the cells (114 of 360) it needs more rounds, which means the tax took back more than the whole gain.

The same happens with no token check at all. In one test (R1-Distill-Llama-8B, 200 MATH-500 problems), a sparse-attention method chose a small set of KV entries to read and reused that choice for four steps. It lost 16.5 points of accuracy and its answers were 61% longer; 31 of the 200 runs hit the length cap. Choosing the entries again at every step removed two thirds of the accuracy loss.

Weight quantization also makes reasoning longer. How much longer depends on the quantization method, even when every method uses 4 bits per weight.

Models also differ in how they react. The authors of the first study ran a control with no approximation at all: they only raised the sampling temperature. Every token still comes from the full model, but the model now picks low-probability tokens more often, which is also what a relaxed check lets through. One model wrote 27% more. The other barely moved.

What the search did not find

“They do not show why an unlikely token makes a reasoning model think more rather than less.”Acceptance Is Not Speed, arXiv 2610.23818, Sec 3.7, on its own results

The effect is published many times. The mechanism is not. After the checks, three parts stayed at least partly open. The checking agents' bets on them run from about 2 to 1 up to 4 to 1, so none is a coin flip.

Part A. Does only the dose matter, or also what the model could not see?

Call the dose how often a method changes a token and how unlikely the changed token is under the full model. Take three ways to disturb a model (sparse attention, random token swaps, a higher temperature) and tune them to the same dose. If all three lengthen the reasoning by the same amount, then the dose, which can be measured without generating anything, predicts the tax of any lossy method. If sparse attention is worse at the same dose, the harm comes from the earlier text that sparse attention cannot see, and the fix is a better choice of what it sees.

One paper suggests that the kind of token matters and not only the dose. Under quantization, at uncertain positions the model picks hesitation words such as “wait”, “but” and “alternatively” more often than it should. Boosting those words lengthens the reasoning; boosting random tokens does not (arXiv 2606.00206).

Part B. Does the tax stop when the errors stop?

Part C. Does the harm travel through the token or through the cache?

An approximate step leaves two things behind. Later steps read both.

One result from the kv-fetch logs shows how large the problem can be, though it does not separate the two channels. On AIME (30 problems, 8 samples each, Qwen3-4B, output cap of 16,384 tokens), letting each step read only 512 cached tokens lowered accuracy from 48.3% to 33.3%. Most of the loss was answers cut off at the cap. The cap is tight in this test: the full model itself hits it in 41% of samples.

What could be built

Today there are two ends. Exact checking verifies every token and pays no length tax. Accept-all verifies none. If Parts A to C show that a small, recognisable set of tokens causes the tax, the design in the middle is selective verification: run the cheap path, and check or roll back only where it matters. If the harm is in the cache, the design is a cheap periodic KV rewrite. Either one has to beat exact checking end to end, which is a high bar because SparseSpec is already exact and pays no tax.

What could sink it

  • It is crowded. At least five 2026 preprints measure the effect. A full search of prior work has to come first, because the searches behind this page were stopped early.
  • The headline is weaker than it first looks. For reasoning models in the main study, the mean tax (+23%) is smaller than the mean gain (+30%), and the median case is a small net win. The interesting cases are the third where the tax wins, and the unchecked methods (sparse attention, quantization) where there is no check to fall back on.
  • Noise. One paper reports that numerical noise alone (GPU count, batch size) changed a reasoning model's response length by up to 9,000 tokens (arXiv 2506.09501). Every arm needs a noise-only control.
  • The checking agents lean one way on each part. They would bet 4 to 1 or better against a single dose curve (Part A), about 2 to 1 toward lasting damage (Part B), and about 2.5 to 1 that the current step's sparse read, not the stored KV, carries most of the harm (Part C). One of them would also bet about 3 to 1 that tokens to which the full model gives very low probability carry most of the extra length. If that is right, a simple filter on such tokens removes most of the tax, and the result is not surprising.

First experiment: about 4 GPU-days on models that fit one 48 GB card.

Question 2

Is there a set of positions where every draft fails?

In a self-draft the model drafts for itself with a cheaper copy of its own computation. In the example below the draft reads only about 5% of the KV cache. Its first drafted token is accepted about 95% of the time. The sixth token of the same draft is accepted less often.

Separately trained draft models end up with similar speedups. One study retrained six drafter recipes under one protocol. For the four drafters it compares before and after, most of the gap between best and worst disappeared.

That leaves the question of where the remaining rejections sit. One possibility is that some of them sit at positions set by the target model, where any approximation fails.

  • If the shared part dominates, acceptance has a ceiling set by the target. Better drafts give little, and the effort should go to spotting those positions early.
  • If the method-specific part dominates, drafts with different weaknesses can be stacked.

Status. The search first asked this for two draft types at a time (for example: do a sparse draft and a 4-bit draft fail at the same tokens?). For any one pair an expert can guess the answer, so those versions were rated as too predictable, and the two that were checked in depth (trained drafters, sparse self-drafts) did not survive. Measuring the shared part over many draft types at once, as in the sketch, was proposed only by the critic at the end. Nobody has searched for earlier work on it yet.

Question 3

What decides when a reasoning model stops?

A reasoning model often settles on its final answer well before it stops writing. With hindsight, one study (Qwen3.5-9B) could have stopped every trace at the point after which its answer no longer changed. That uses 35–44% of the tokens overall and keeps every answer. Rules that must decide during generation either stop later or lose accuracy. DEER shortens the output by 19–43% on average. In the hindsight study itself, a simple rule (stop when three checks in a row agree) gets down to 45% of the tokens on the hard set but costs 9.2 points of accuracy.

A natural project is a better signal for when to stop. The checks give a reason to be careful. A probe is a small classifier that reads the model's internal state and predicts whether the final answer will be correct. An early probe looks good over many problems, but it mostly detects how hard the problem is.

Status. Many groups already work on this. Part of the gap exists by definition, because the hindsight point is defined with the trace's own final answer. And a stop signal built on an early probe would mostly separate easy problems from hard ones; it would not find the moment when the answer is ready. A narrow question remains: on problems where the model's own attempts disagree, does the text so far say anything about the final answer that the question alone does not?

Question 4

Why does reading fewer bytes not buy time?

Sparse attention reads less of the KV cache, so it should be faster in proportion. On a real GPU kernel at batch size 1, the gain flattens early. At batch size 16 the same kernel keeps scaling.

Related numbers from work you know (not all in the same regime):

  • FlashInfer (arXiv 2501.01005, Table 5): at batch 16, the shared-prefix kernel reads the prefix once instead of 16 times. With a 1,024-token prefix it is still only 3% faster (45.17 µs against 46.52 µs). With an 8,192-token prefix it is 2.6 times faster (88.67 µs against 226.57 µs).
  • SparseSpec (arXiv 2512.01278): attention time falls 3.29×, from 17.1 ms to 5.2 ms. If time followed the bytes read, the paper's own acceptance and sparsity figures would give about 5.1× (the search agent's arithmetic). The paper calls 3.29× consistent with its estimate, and this is a large-batch regime with other explanations (tile shape, 9-token verification queries, top-K bookkeeping), so it is not evidence for a fixed cost per call.
  • The SubSpec notes (subspec_v2): a fused single-launch cascade kernel was 1.15–1.37× faster than the multi-level cascade kernel when timed alone, and about 2% slower end to end, which is inside the run-to-run spread. Attention was under 1% of the forward pass and the extra planning call cancelled the gain. This one is about attention's share of step time, not about bytes read.

A probable cause at small batch is a fixed cost for every kernel call (starting the kernel, merging partial results, a minimum amount of memory traffic) that stays the same however few KV entries are read. The checking agent guessed 10–50 µs per call. Nobody has measured it for this case.

Status. Predictable, and it reads as engineering; the check left no narrower version. The critic (not verified) suggested merging it with two other kernel questions into a cost model that screens the other three questions: before any accuracy work, estimate what share of step time a design can touch on a 48 GB card.
