# Eight published setups already fit a four-GPU node

A PhD student with one node of four idle 48 GB GPUs does not need to invent a problem: the 2024-2026 top-venue literature contains at least eight evaluation setups that ran on that class of hardware or reduce to it, ship code, and name their own unfinished work. The strongest three are **SpecReason** (NeurIPS'25; two A6000-48GB cards on vLLM 0.8.2), **DiffKV** (SOSP'25; one, two or four L40 48 GB cards, built on vLLM) and the **IISWC'26 agentic workload characterization** (27B and 31B models on vLLM at tensor-parallel degree two). Behind them sit SPEX, LMetric, JITServe, BatchLLM and Nixie, each of which misses one criterion. Hardware match alone is a poor filter: Parrot and mLoRA were both evaluated on four A6000s, and both lines are now crowded. The catalogue covers roughly 160 entries across six areas, and the pattern that separates open setups from closed ones is consistent: the open ones sit where a model-level technique meets a batched serving engine, or where a new workload (agents, reasoning, offline batch) has been measured but not yet scheduled for. Four directions the student already tested and dropped recur in the catalogue and are marked where they appear. Every hardware-fit verdict below is the note-takers' reading of reported testbeds, not a test; several venues were confirmed only from paper headers; and every follow-up count is a lower bound.

## How to read the verdicts

The student's node has about four idle 48 GB cards (RTX A6000 / RTX 6000 Ada) on PCIe, no H100 and no NVLink, and can hold models up to about 30B. The student works mainly in vLLM and knows its scheduler and KV-cache code. A setup is called a fit when the paper's own testbed is that class of hardware, "scaled down" when the paper used a larger cluster but its smaller models and the mechanism under test would run on four cards, and "no" when the result depends on hardware the node lacks (RDMA, NVLink, 80 GB cards, phones). The node's host DRAM and NVMe speed are not known to this report, and they decide the fit of every offload paper.

Four directions are closed for this student and are flagged throughout: **(D1)** selecting a subset of KV pages at decode time for long reasoning outputs, in the style of Quest, ShadowKV and InfiniGen; **(D2)** state checkpoints and prefix caching for hybrid linear-attention models in vLLM, where a perfect policy gained only 2.6-5.5% on the Mooncake traces; **(D3)** profiling vLLM 0.32 for single-GPU bottlenecks, where decode already runs at 89-95% of the hardware limit; and **(D4)** accelerating RL post-training rollouts, which a separate earlier report covers.

## Serving and scheduling is the deepest catalogue, and its open slot is the router

Serving and scheduling holds at least 26 in-scope papers, concentrated at OSDI'24, SOSP'24, ATC'25, OSDI'25, SOSP'25, NSDI'26, ASPLOS'26 and OSDI'26 ([OSDI'24](https://www.usenix.org/conference/osdi24/technical-sessions), [NSDI'26](https://www.usenix.org/conference/nsdi26/technical-sessions), [ASPLOS'26](https://www.asplos-conference.org/asplos2026/program/), [OSDI'26](https://www.usenix.org/conference/osdi26/technical-sessions)). The shared evaluation recipe is stable: one to four open-weight models between 7B and 70B, ShareGPT or LMSYS-Chat-1M prompts with Poisson or Azure-trace arrivals, vLLM as the baseline, and capacity or goodput under a P99 time-to-first-token or time-between-tokens target.

### Single-engine scheduling is reproducible but crowded

**Sarathi-Serve** (OSDI'24) is the reference point. It introduced chunked prefill with stall-free batching, evaluated Mistral-7B on one A100, Yi-34B on two A100s and LLaMA2-70B on eight A40 48 GB cards, against vLLM and Orca, on openchat_sharegpt4 and arxiv_summarization, and reported 2.6x to 3.7x higher capacity under a P99 time-between-tokens target ([Sarathi-Serve](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.02310)). Its code is public and the 7B and 34B configurations fit the node. The authors leave two things open in their own words: "dynamically varying the token budget based on workload characteristics... We leave this exploration for future work" and "We leave a quantitative comparison between Sarathi-Serve and disaggregation-based solutions for future work." Neither is a safe opening, because Sarathi is now the baseline in QoServe, JITServe, MuxWise, Bullet and FastServe, and the token-budget question is the first thing each of them touches.

The length-prediction line has the same shape. **Learning to Rank** (NeurIPS'24, venue confirmed at search level only) schedules by predicted relative output length on vLLM v0.4.1, with Llama-3 8B and 70B on ShareGPT and LMSYS-Chat-1M, against FCFS, MLFQ and S3, and reports 2.8x lower latency; its code is public and the 8B runs fit ([Learning to Rank](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.15792)). FastServe (NSDI'26) adds preemptive multi-level queues but ships no code and used two eight-A100 nodes ([FastServe](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/nsdi26-wu-bingyang.pdf)). Output-length uncertainty is named as unsolved by five separate papers in this area, and DeepServe states it most plainly: "Accurately predicting decode lengths with minimal overhead remains a challenging research problem" ([DeepServe](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.14417)). That many independent statements make it a real problem and also a contested one.

**VTC** (OSDI'24) is the least crowded of the single-engine papers. It defines token-weighted fair queuing for LLM serving, ran Llama-2-7b on an A10G 24 GB and 13b on an A100, replayed a 27-client, ten-minute LMSYS Arena trace against FCFS, request-per-minute limits and least-counter-first, and ships its artifact ([VTC](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.00588)). It leaves preemption open ("we leave an investigation on preemption as an orthogonal future work") and concedes that its cost formula "cannot accurately reflect the actual LLM serving cost". The only follow-up the notes found is the DLPM policy inside SkyWalker ([SkyWalker](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.24095)). The weakness is that VTC runs on S-LoRA/LightLLM, not vLLM, and its trace is small.

**QoServe** (ASPLOS'26) and **JITServe** (NSDI'26) are the current front. QoServe serves three QoS tiers on Llama3-8B (one A100) and Qwen-7B (two A100s) with ShareGPT and the Azure 2024 conversation and code traces, gains 32% capacity over Sarathi variants, and has no stated code release, no limitations section and no follow-ups found ([QoServe](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.22562)). JITServe is the better starting point: it ships code, evaluates Llama-3.1-8B, Qwen2.5-14B and Qwen3-30B-A3B (all within reach of the node) plus a 70B model on 16 A100s, mixes chatbot, math-reasoning, deep-research and agentic-code workloads with Azure-trace arrivals, compares against Autellix, Learning to Rank, Sarathi and vLLM, and reports 1.4x to 6.3x goodput ([JITServe](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.20068)). Its authors name three gaps: goodput is all-or-nothing, distribution shifts "may require retraining predictors or incorporating explicit fallback policies", and mixed-GPU and multi-replica profiling is not handled. No follow-up was found, which for a 2026 paper mostly reflects its age.

### Engine-level throughput work collides with the student's own profiling

**Bullet** (ASPLOS'26) runs prefill and decode concurrently on one GPU, ships code, compares against vLLM v0.8.5, SGLang v0.4.6 and NanoFlow on Llama3.1-8B and 70B, and reports 1.26x average throughput on A100, H100 and H20 cards ([Bullet](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.19516)). Its authors state that "on low-compute GPUs running dense models, the limited SM budget" tempers the benefit, and that "wave quantization remains an open issue". MuxWise (ASPLOS'26) takes a similar route on SGLang with eight-GPU nodes and only plans to release code ([MuxWise](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.14489)). These are the catalogued papers nearest to dropped direction D3. The student's finding that vLLM 0.32 decode already sits at 89-95% of the hardware limit on one GPU argues against adopting either: the headroom these papers exploit is on prefill-decode interference, and on an A6000-class card the authors themselves expect the gain to shrink.

### Disaggregation and cold start need a cluster the node is not

DistServe (OSDI'24) used 32 A100s on NVLink and OPT models up to 175B ([DistServe](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.09670)); Splitwise (ISCA'24) contributes a CPU-runnable simulator and the Azure traces ([Splitwise](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.18677), [AzurePublicDataset](https://github.com/Azure/AzurePublicDataset)); LoongServe (SOSP'24) used eight A800s ([LoongServe](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.09526)). Six papers name disaggregation scheduling, scaling or fault tolerance as open, so the problem is live, but a four-card PCIe node can only reproduce a shrunken version and the line is crowded. The serverless cold-start line is in the same position. **ServerlessLLM** (OSDI'24) is a hardware fit, since one of its testbeds was four servers of four A40 48 GB cards and its single-server results stand alone, and it ships code ([ServerlessLLM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.14351)). It is also the baseline in BlitzScale, HydraServe, Aegaeon, Prism and WarmServe, and BlitzScale needs RDMA ([BlitzScale](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.17246), [HydraServe](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.15524), [Prism](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.04021)). Multi-LoRA serving offers one hardware match, **Chameleon** (MICRO'25, search-level venue), whose main testbed is a single A40 48 GB running Llama-7B/13B/30B on S-LoRA, but the notes found no code link and no limitations section ([Chameleon](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.17741)).

### Cluster routing over prefix-hash traces is the open slot

**Llumnix** (OSDI'24) reschedules requests across vLLM instances by live migration, on 16 A10 24 GB cards over PCIe, with ShareGPT and BurstGPT, and ships code ([Llumnix](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.03243)). It admits "a lot of heuristics to explore for setting the virtual usage" and supports only two priority classes. **LMetric** (OSDI'26, venue verified) is its sharpest successor and the best setup in this area. Its router scores each instance by the product of the request's new prefill tokens and the instance's batch size, and with that one rule cuts time-to-first-token by 92% and 39% and time-per-output-token by 24% and 51% against the Bailian production scheduler, the vLLM router, ai-Dynamo and llm-d ([LMetric](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/osdi26-zhang-dingyan.pdf)). The testbed is 16 H20 96 GB cards with **one vLLM instance per GPU**, serving Qwen2-7B and Qwen3-30B MoE, which reduces naturally to four instances on four cards. The router and two of the four traces are public ([Bailian traces](https://github.com/alibaba-edu/qwen-bailian-usagetraces-anon)); the Coder trace is not released. The authors concede that "there do exist cases where multiplication may fail due to extremely skewed KV$ accesses", that "we cannot afford to sweep all possible configurations", and that "Efficient scheduling for PD-disaggregated serving is beyond the scope of this paper." No follow-up outside the authors' group was found.

The traces matter as much as the systems. Only the Mooncake FAST'25 release and the Bailian release carry prefix-hash information, which is what a KV-aware router or cache policy needs for replay ([Mooncake traces](https://github.com/kvcache-ai/Mooncake/tree/main/FAST25-release)). BurstGPT supplies arrival burstiness ([BurstGPT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.17644)), and ServeGen (NSDI'26) supplies a generator fitted to production and shows that ShareGPT with Poisson arrivals under-provisions by 50% ([ServeGen](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.09999)). A paper that reruns a 2024 scheduling result under ServeGen or Bailian arrivals has a defensible contribution before it adds any mechanism. Vidur (MLSys'24) gives a CPU simulator with under 9% error for sweeping configurations the node cannot hold ([Vidur](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.05465)).

## KV-cache systems fit the node only when the cache stays on the GPU

The engine-memory notes catalogue 20 main and 8 secondary papers, and vLLM hosts more of them than any other engine: DiffKV, the SOSP'25 Jenga allocator, vAttention, CacheSlide, CacheBlend and PrefillOnly are all built on it, while Strata, ECHO and Prism are built on SGLang. For a student fluent in vLLM's KV-cache code this is the area with the lowest porting cost. It is also the area where two of the four dropped directions live.

### Offload hierarchies are crowded and depend on the host, not the GPU

**Strata** (OSDI'26) is the current reference for hierarchical KV caching. It runs on SGLang with Llama-3.1-8B, Qwen2.5-14B-1M and a 70B model over LooGLE, NarrativeQA, ReviewMT and ShareGPT, on eight H200s with 1.6 TB of DRAM and 7 GB/s NVMe, against vLLM v0.8.5, vLLM with LMCache, TensorRT-LLM and SGLang-HiCache, and reports up to 5x ([Strata](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.18572)). The 8B and 14B models fit the node; the DRAM and NVMe do not obviously scale down, and the node's host configuration is unknown. Strata's authors state that cache-aware batching "can still treat requests unevenly, risking SLO violations for individual requests". The line is crowded: ECHO sits in the same OSDI'26 session, and KVDrive and Swarm followed on arXiv, alongside a survey of more than 30 such systems ([KVDrive](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.18071), [Swarm](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.17803), [survey](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.02574)). ECHO needs a 671B model on eight H20s, and DirectKV needs GH200 hardware and says outright that "on PCIe-only platforms... throughput benefits are often limited by interconnect bandwidth" ([ECHO](https://www.usenix.org/conference/osdi26/presentation/liu-guangda), [DirectKV](https://www.usenix.org/conference/osdi26/presentation/luo)). That sentence is a published warning against doing tiered-KV work on this node.

KunServe (EuroSys'26, header only) drops parameters under memory pressure and beats vLLM v0.6.3, InferCept and Llumnix on BurstGPT by 72.2x in tail time-to-first-token, with code, but on clusters of 80 GB cards and with the admission that "Our current implementation focuses on dense models" ([KunServe](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.18169)). CacheSlide (FAST'26) ships code on vLLM 0.8.5 for position-shifted reuse and Bidaw (FAST'26) releases an interactive-conversation trace but no system code; both used single 80 GB cards and scale down ([CacheSlide](https://www.usenix.org/conference/fast26/presentation/liu-yang), [Bidaw](https://www.usenix.org/conference/fast26/presentation/hu-shipeng)).

### DiffKV is the one compression system evaluated on 48 GB cards inside vLLM

**DiffKV** (SOSP'25; the title is on the accepted list and the facts here come from the arXiv version) compresses keys, values, tokens and heads at different rates, which leaves each request with an irregular memory footprint and so requires an on-GPU memory manager with parallel compaction. It is implemented on vLLM in about 4.5K lines of CUDA/C++ and 9K of Python, and evaluated on **NVIDIA L40 48 GB cards: one for 7B/8B models, two for 32B, four for 70B** ([DiffKV](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.03131)). Models are Llama3-8B/70B, Qwen2.5-7B/32B, QwQ-32B and two R1 distillations; benchmarks span MMLU, GSM8K, MATH, HumanEval+, MBPP+, LongBench, AIME24 and GPQA; baselines are vLLM, Quest and SnapKV. It compresses the KV cache 2.7x to 5.7x and raises throughput 1.9x to 5.4x. Code is public. The authors leave two items: precision policies with more than two levels ("we leave the exploration of such flexible policies for future work") and grouped-query attention with higher queries-per-KV ratios, "which we leave to future investigation". The notes found no 2026 paper using DiffKV as a baseline.

DiffKV is adjacent to dropped direction D1, and the student should be precise about how. Quest is one of its baselines and token pruning is one of its levers, so its accuracy story overlaps with page selection. Its systems contribution, the memory manager for irregular allocations inside vLLM's paged KV cache, does not. **Rethinking KV Cache Compression** (MLSys'25) supplies the matching critique and a second hardware match: "The majority of the experiments are conducted on A6000 GPUs", it re-measures KIVI, GEAR, StreamingLLM and H2O on ShareGPT and LongBench, and its code is public ([Rethinking KV compression](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.24000)). Together they form a reproducible pair: a critique that says compression gains shrink under realistic serving, and a system that claims to have fixed that inside vLLM.

### Non-prefix reuse and the "beyond dense attention" problem

**CacheBlend** (EuroSys'25, venue unverified) reuses KV for retrieved chunks that are not prefixes by selectively recomputing tokens. Its testbed is **two A40 48 GB cards** with 128 GB of RAM and 1 TB of NVMe, running Mistral-7B, Yi-34B and an 8-bit Llama-70B on 2WikiMQA and Musique, and its code lives in LMCache ([CacheBlend](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.16444)). It is a hardware fit with public code, and it is already a baseline in DroidSpeak and CacheSlide, so it fails the crowding criterion ([DroidSpeak](https://www.usenix.org/conference/nsdi26/presentation/liu-yuhan)).

The most widely stated open problem in this area is extending KV-cache systems beyond dense attention. Strata writes that "As hybrid mechanisms such as sparse and linear attention gain adoption, extending the same level of support... raises distinct challenges in both memory management and scheduling-policy design"; CacheBlend leaves "architectures other than transformer such as Mamba and Griffin for future work"; the SOSP'25 Jenga allocator adds Mamba and Jamba support to vLLM without a code link ([Jenga](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.18292)). **Marconi** (MLSys'25) is the one system paper that addresses it, with public code and trace-driven token hit rates ([Marconi](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.19379)). **This is dropped direction D2.** The follow-ups the notes found are engineering only: SGLang's MambaRadixCache, the SegLen blog, and a vLLM pull request and issue ([SGLang blog](https://pytorch.org/blog/hybrid-models-meet-sglang-more-than-full-attention/), [SegLen](https://abdelfattah-lab.github.io/blogs/seglen/), [vLLM PR 46384](https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/pull/46384), [vLLM issue 40696](https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/issues/40696)). The thin follow-up record is consistent with the student's own result that a perfect policy gains only 2.6-5.5% on the Mooncake traces: authors keep naming the problem, and the public traces do not reward solving it. **InfiniGen** (OSDI'24) sits squarely in dropped direction D1 ([InfiniGen](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.19707)).

Two further gaps recur. IMPRESS (FAST'25) built its own data "Due to the lack of open-source, real-world datasets for prefix reuse", and HCache and Pensieve ship no code ([IMPRESS](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/fast25-chen-weijian-impress.pdf), [HCache](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.05004), [Pensieve](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.05516)). vAttention (ASPLOS'25) ships code but on vLLM v0.2.7, which is too old to rebase cheaply ([vAttention](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.04437)).

## Agent and reasoning workloads are measured better than they are served

The notes hold 18 peer-reviewed and 3 arXiv-only papers here, extracted from roughly the first 100,000 characters of each arXiv HTML page, so a few numbers differ between abstract and body. The area splits cleanly: program-aware scheduling is the most crowded line in the whole catalogue, while reasoning-serving and workload-characterization papers from 2026 have almost no successors yet.

### Program-aware scheduling: exact hardware match, no room

**Parrot** (OSDI'24) exposes application-level structure to the serving layer through semantic variables. One of its two testbeds is **a server with four A6000 48 GB cards**, it runs LLaMA 13B and 7B against LangChain with FastChat and vLLM, and it reports up to 11.7x ([Parrot](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.19888)). It states its own limit: it "only supports cloud-side orchestration of LLM requests without involving dynamic control flow". The repository URL was not confirmed by the notes. **Autellix** (NSDI'26, published as Agentix) schedules whole programs with a least-attained-service queue on eight A100-80GB cards against vLLM v0.6.1 and MLFQ, gains 4x to 15x, ships no code, and admits "a noticable gap remains between Autellix and SRPT" ([Autellix](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.13965)). That gap has attracted Continuum, ThunderAgent, MARS, HexAGenT, AgentServeSim, KAIROS and Nalar within about a year ([Continuum](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.02230v2), [ThunderAgent](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.13692), [MARS](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.26963v1)). ThunderAgent used eight H100s and also targets RL rollout throughput, which puts that part of it in dropped direction D4. Parrot shows that criterion (a) by itself selects for nothing: the hardware is the student's, and the problem is taken.

Two neighbours are less contested. **InferCept** (ICML'24) handles requests paused on tool calls, compares against vLLM's discard, preserve and swap policies, gains 1.6x to 2x, and ships code ([InferCept](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01869v2)). **Pie** (SOSP'25) makes the serving loop programmable, runs Llama 3 1B/3B/8B on **one L4 24 GB**, beats vLLM v0.6.0, SGLang v0.4.4 and LMQL by 1.3x to 3.4x on agentic workloads, and ships code ([Pie](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.24051v1)). Pie's authors name multi-GPU-node load balancing ("distributed coordination, globally-aware scheduling, and cache-aware placement policies") and contention policies such as quotas and KV-page swapping as "promising future work". Pie fits the node with room to spare; the cost is that it is its own engine, so the student's vLLM fluency transfers only in concept.

### RAG serving mostly lacks code or needs H100s

METIS (SOSP'25) matches the hardware, with two A40s and Mistral-7B AWQ, but ships no code and depends on a proprietary GPT-4o profiler ([METIS](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.10543)). HedraRAG (SOSP'25) used H100s ([HedraRAG](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.09138)), and RAGO (ISCA'25 per its repository) is a simulator ([RAGO](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.14649)). **TeleRAG** (MLSys'26, paper-stated venue) is the usable one: it ships code and data, serves Llama-3.2-3B, Llama-3-8B and Mistral-Small-22B, and its single-GPU configuration on an RTX 4090 carries over, with CPU Faiss plus SGLang as the baseline ([TeleRAG](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.20969)). Its stated limits are that it covers only IVF indexes and "We leave the exploration of different parallelisms for future work".

### Reasoning serving: SPEX and PASCAL have no successors found yet

**SPEX** (OSDI'26) attacks "a synchronization bottleneck caused by sequential reward-guided exploration" in tree-of-thought search by speculatively expanding nodes before their rewards return. It is built on SGLang, ships code, runs Llemma-7B/34B, DeepSeek-R1-8B and Qwen3-30B-A3B over GSM8K, MATH-500, AIME 2024/2025, BRUMO and HMMT with four search algorithms, and reports 1.2x to 3x, or about 4.1x combined with token-level speculative decoding ([SPEX](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.10195)). **The 7B and 8B models were run on an A6000**; the 34B and 30B models were run on A100s, and the paper does not give card counts. It has no limitations section, but the body concedes that it "struggles to achieve significant acceleration" on skewed trees, that the speculation hit rate "decreases as the prediction distance increases", and that it becomes compute-bound at larger batch sizes. No follow-up was found, which may only reflect its date.

**Dynasor** (NeurIPS'25) allocates compute across reasoning queries by a certainty proxy, on SGLang v0.3.3, saving up to 50% of compute, with code ([Dynasor](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.20993)). It says it "did not explore its integration with advanced serving techniques like PD disaggregation or chunked prefill", and it already has at least three follow-ups, SPEX among them. **PASCAL** (HPCA'26, author-stated venue) schedules the thinking and answering phases separately for R1-Distill-Qwen-32B on vLLM v0.6.1, cuts tail time-to-first-token by 72% against FCFS and round-robin, and leaves phase resource partitioning "for future work"; it has no code, used one H100 plus a simulator, and derives its lengths from o4-mini ([PASCAL](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.11530)). PASCAL is a clear problem statement on the student's engine with no successor found, and a poor reproduction target because nothing is released.

### Two characterization studies hand over an unserved workload

**The Cost of Dynamic Reasoning** (HPCA'26, author-stated venue) runs CoT, ReAct, Reflexion, LATS and LLMCompiler agents on HotpotQA, WebShop, MATH and HumanEval against vLLM 0.6.6 with prefix caching, using Llama-3.1-8B on one A100 40 GB and 70B on eight, and ships its harness ([Cost of Dynamic Reasoning](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.04301)). It finds GPUs idle up to 54.5% of the time and tools taking 30.2% of agent latency on average, and it says plainly: "A detailed exploration of efficient agent serving under SLA constraints is left as future work".

**Agentic AI Workload Characteristics** (IISWC'26, author-stated venue) is closer still to the student's stack. It serves **Qwen3.6-27B and Gemma4-31B on vLLM v0.20.0 at tensor-parallel degree two**, instrumented with OpenTelemetry, and drives them with Claude Code through Harbor on ADE-Bench, DABStep, GAIA, SWE-bench Pro and Terminal-Bench 2.0 ([Agentic AI Workload Characteristics](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.26297)). Source and data are archived ([source](https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.33188466.v1), [data](https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.33188042.v1)). Without cache thrashing, prefix-cache hit rates are 84.6-99.5% and decode takes 91.0-98.6% of LLM time; LLM inference is 71-98% of end-to-end runtime. **Under cache thrashing the hit rate falls from about 98% to 16-26% and throughput drops by up to 66%**, and failed runs carry contexts up to 1.8x those of successful ones. The testbed is two H100 NVL cards, so criterion (a) is a near miss: tensor-parallel two maps onto two 48 GB cards, but with less KV headroom the thrashing thresholds will move, and that shift is itself a measurable result. The authors leave speculative tool execution, context compaction and tree search uncharacterized. No follow-up was found.

The cross-cutting open problem, stated by five independent sources across this area and the KV-cache area, is what to do with state held on the GPU while an agent waits on a tool or a user. An arXiv-only study adds that OS-level execution is 56-74% of task latency for coding agents ([AgentCgroup](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.09345)), and a production Copilot study says its trace release is only "planned" ([Copilot study](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.00101)). Public agent traces with timing remain missing, which makes the IISWC'26 artifact more valuable than its venue suggests.

## Model-level efficiency papers fit the hardware and stop at batch size one

The model-level notes confirm 45 papers through OpenReview venue strings; reviewer-stated weaknesses were not obtained. This area has the most 48 GB-class testbeds in the catalogue: SWIFT on four A6000s, SpecReason on two, LServe, QServe and Hogwild! on an L40S, and dKV-Cache on an A6000. It also has a protocol split that defines the opportunity. Speculative-decoding and diffusion-LM papers default to batch size one in a research codebase with no serving engine; reasoning-efficiency and serving-quantization papers use vLLM, SGLang or TensorRT-LLM with batching. Numbers across the two groups are not comparable, and "works at batch one, unclear inside a batched engine" is stated in four separate lines of work.

### Speculative decoding is crowded, and its batched-serving gap is documented

**SWIFT** (ICLR'25) is the hardware match: layer-skipping self-speculation on **four RTX A6000s**, LLaMA-2 13B/70B and CodeLLaMA 13B/34B, batch one in HF transformers, 1.3x to 1.6x, code public, with CAS-Spec (NeurIPS'25) and Vegas (ICML'26) already following ([SWIFT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.06916)). EAGLE-3 (NeurIPS'25) supplies the number that matters to a serving researcher: inside SGLang on an H100, EAGLE falls below the no-speculation baseline from batch size 24, and EAGLE-3's gain is 1.38x at batch 64 ([EAGLE-3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.01840)). Spec-Bench lists "How to apply Speculative Decoding in batched inference scenarios?" as an open challenge and itself measures Vicuna-7B on one RTX 3090 at batch one ([Spec-Bench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.07851)). SuffixDecoding (NeurIPS'25 spotlight) is the exception that ships inside vLLM through ArcticInference ([SuffixDecoding](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.04975)); one of its MLSys'26 follow-ups, on distribution-aware speculative decoding for RL training, falls in dropped direction D4. With at least ten ICLR and ICML 2026 records, the method space is saturated. The batched-engine measurement is the only part with room, and it is a measurement paper, not a new method.

### Sparse attention and reasoning-KV compression are the student's dropped ground

**LServe** (MLSys'25), **ShadowKV** (ICML'25), **ThinKV** (ICLR'26 Oral) and **R-KV** (NeurIPS'25) all sit in or beside dropped direction D1. LServe does Quest-style page selection and has an L40S 48 GB as its second testbed ([LServe](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.14866)); ShadowKV is named in the dropped direction itself ([ShadowKV](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.21465)). ThinKV compresses the KV cache at decode time for reasoning models to under 5% of its size with up to 5.8x throughput, ships no code, says it is "not directly applicable to settings dominated by long input contexts", and already has BeaconKV and a head-importance study at ICML'26 ([ThinKV](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.01290), [BeaconKV](https://openreview.net/forum?id=bgAbffQCrL)). R-KV keeps 10% of the KV for R1 distillations and reports 6.6x throughput from larger batches, but at batch one shows only "a slight throughput advantage over FullKV" and is not compatible with paged attention ([R-KV](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.24133)). R-KV's incompatibility with paged attention is exactly the systems gap DiffKV claims to close, which is one more reason DiffKV is the better entry point than any of these. KVzip (NeurIPS'25 oral) is very crowded ([KVzip](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.23416)), and SCBench (ICLR'25) reports that sub-linear-memory methods degrade in multi-turn use ([SCBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.10319)).

### SpecReason is the closest published match to the student's node

**SpecReason** (NeurIPS'25 poster) lets a small model speculate whole reasoning steps while the base model scores each step with a short prefill-only pass. The base models are QwQ-32B and Skywork-OR1-Preview-32B, the small ones DeepSeek-R1-1.5B and Zyphra ZR1-1.5B, on AIME, MATH500 and GPQA ([SpecReason](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.07891)). The paper's hardware statement reads: "two NVIDIA A6000-48GB GPUs. We use vLLM 0.8.2 ... enable prefix caching. Both models are served with a tensor parallelism degree of two." It reports 1.4x to 3.0x speedup with accuracy changes of +0.4 to +9.0%, and a further 8.8-58.0% latency reduction when combined with token-level speculative decoding. Code is public ([repository](https://github.com/ruipeterpan/specreason)). The authors say verification offers "only a coarse-grained configuration space" and that "Future work can explore more sophisticated strategies, such as logprob-based confidence estimates or dynamic thresholds, to enable finer-grained tradeoffs without incurring additional runtime cost". Two follow-ups were found, Speculative Thinking (COLM 2025) and a "Scaling Up, Speeding Up" benchmark (ICLR 2026) ([Speculative Thinking](https://openreview.net/forum?id=4Ns18bSoHo), [benchmark](https://openreview.net/forum?id=DjOmnwX4wJ)).

SpecReason is evaluated one request at a time. Its mechanism, two models sharing two cards with a prefill-only scoring pass and prefix caching, is a scheduling and KV-cache problem as soon as requests arrive concurrently, and that is the student's home ground. Other reasoning-efficiency entries are weaker starts: DEER (ICLR'26) runs on vLLM with code and is very crowded ([DEER](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.15895)), and Thinkless needs four H100s for training ([Thinkless](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.13379)).

### Parallel decoding, diffusion LMs and quantization

**Hogwild! Inference** (NeurIPS'25 spotlight) runs two to four QwQ-32B workers over one concurrently updated KV cache, times it on a single L40S with an AWQ model, and ships code ([Hogwild!](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.06261)). The authors report "reduced robustness when applied to smaller models or longer contexts" and that "our automatic evaluation metric relies on a proprietary model". No follow-up was confirmed, but that search was rate-limited. It is the most unusual KV-cache workload in the catalogue and has no serving-engine implementation.

Diffusion LMs repeat the speculative-decoding pattern a year later. Fast-dLLM (ICLR'26) reaches 27.6x on one A100 at batch one and concedes that at larger batch it "struggles to match LLaMA"; it has at least six follow-ups ([Fast-dLLM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.22618)). dKV-Cache (NeurIPS'25) measures speed on an A6000 with code and leaves system-level optimization open ([dKV-Cache](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.15781)). In quantization, QServe (MLSys'25) is the serving-grade entry, with an L40S 48 GB as one of two testbeds and TensorRT-LLM as baseline ([QServe](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.04532)); FlatQuant (ICML'25) notes that "the optimal selection of calibration sets remains an open question" ([FlatQuant](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.09426)). The hybrid-architecture papers are model-level neighbours of dropped direction D2: Jet-Nemotron needs 350B training tokens though its inference fits, and Mamba in the Llama found that speculative decoding gave 1.5x on an RTX 3090 and no gain on an H100 until optimized ([Jet-Nemotron](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.15884), [Mamba in the Llama](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.15237)).

## Constrained-hardware papers assume one small GPU and one user

The notes hold 23 peer-reviewed papers here, 18 with confirmed venues. Most target a 12-24 GB consumer card or a phone, so the student's node is over-provisioned for the GPU side and unknown on the host side.

### CPU-GPU hybrid offload: reproducible, sparsity-bound, and re-implemented each round

**PowerInfer** (SOSP'24) keeps hot neurons on the GPU and cold ones on the CPU, on an RTX 4090 24 GB with 192 GB of RAM, at batch one, extending llama.cpp, with 7.23x average speedup ([PowerInfer](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.12456v2)). Its limit is stated outright: "The effectiveness of PowerInfer is directly correlated with the model's activation sparsity"; SiLU models gain only 1.47x to 1.7x, and speedup falls with batch size. **Kairox** (OSDI'26) adds online neuron rebalancing, ships code, and reaches 3.70x over PowerInfer on 12 GB and 24 GB cards ([Kairox](https://www.usenix.org/conference/osdi26/presentation/jiang-yapeng), [code](https://github.com/Anhelor/kairox.cpp)). It says its non-ReLU results "highlight potential opportunities for future research into inference optimization for less sparse or non-sparse model architectures on edge devices". DynamicInfer (ICLR'26) beats PowerInfer by up to 59% without code ([DynamicInfer](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2026/file/0b9762d1f15c68057275fa18d384c1a8-Paper-Conference.pdf)). To reproduce these on a 48 GB card the student must cap GPU memory artificially or move to models too large for the node's stated range, and the line is llama.cpp throughout. The notes observe that the newest paper in each chain re-implements its predecessor, which suggests released code does not run unchanged on current stacks.

**Fiddler** (ICLR'25) is the exact hardware match: Mixtral-8x7B at 16-bit with cold experts on the CPU, and its second environment is **an RTX 6000 Ada 48 GB on PCIe Gen4 with a 112-core Xeon Platinum 8480+** ([Fiddler](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.07033), [code](https://github.com/efeslab/fiddler)). It gains 1.26x at single batch and 11.57x on beam search against llama.cpp, handles only "a small number of concurrent queries", and needs a CPU with AVX512_BF16. It has been followed by MoE-Lightning (ASPLOS'25) and three arXiv-only papers the notes did not read ([MoE-Lightning](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.11217v1), [FreeToken](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.16157), [CoX-MoE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.17889), [TriMoE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.01058)). **NEO** (MLSys'25) offloads decode attention and KV to the CPU, compares against vLLM, gains 26% on an A10G and 14% on an H100, and writes: "We leave as future work integrating Neo into vLLM to measure our 4-GPU and 8-GPU performance gains" ([NEO](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.01142)). That is an open problem phrased for this student, but NEO's code was not located and the modest gain on faster cards predicts a small one on an A6000.

### GPU sharing on one machine is the least crowded line here

**Nixie** (OSDI'26) multiplexes one consumer GPU in time across unmodified applications: Ollama 0.12.11, SGLang 0.5.4, llama.cpp and ComfyUI, with Qwen3 models up to a 30B MoE, on one RTX 5090 32 GB and on eight RTX A5000 24 GB cards ([Nixie](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/osdi26-xu-yechen.pdf)). Against Ollama, UVM, TGS and nvshare it cuts latency up to 3.8x, cuts pinned host memory by 66.8%, and reaches 73% of two-GPU performance on one A5000. Code and the evaluation harness are both public ([Nixie](https://github.com/XOR-op/Nixie), [nixie-eval](https://github.com/XOR-op/nixie-eval)). The authors list three openings: "How to integrate spatial multiplexing into Nixie is a promising future direction", policies that treat "different GPU memory regions (e.g., model weights, activations, KV caches) differently", and semantic hints from applications. No follow-up was found. Whether it can be installed on a shared machine without privileges the student lacks is unchecked.

The cluster-scale version of this problem is taken. MuxServe (ICML'24) used 32 A100s and has been re-implemented by Aegaeon, Prism and WarmServe ([MuxServe](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.02015), [WarmServe](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.09472)). Mobile and edge papers such as llm.npu and HeteroInfer need Snapdragon phones or Jetson boards and are out of reach ([llm.npu](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.05858v2), [HeteroInfer](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.14794)). On energy, the **ML.ENERGY Benchmark** (NeurIPS'25) runs on vLLM with public code, shows that TDP-based estimates overstate energy by up to 4.1x and that tuning cuts energy 44% at a 100 ms time-per-output-token target, and admits "We were not able to run the benchmark multiple times due to the high monetary cost" ([ML.ENERGY](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.06371)). It was run on A100 and H100 cards; nobody in the catalogue has repeated it on 48 GB workstation cards. The notes also verified no top-venue benchmark comparing llama.cpp, MLC and vLLM on a single workstation GPU.

## Fine-tuning and batch systems: multi-LoRA is taken, offline inference lacks shared workloads

Twenty papers are catalogued. The fine-tuning half contains the single most exact hardware match in the report, and it is closed; the offline-inference half has open problems on vLLM and no public workload to evaluate them on.

### Multi-LoRA training matches the node and has five systems in two years

**mLoRA** (PVLDB 2025, artifact badge) trains many LoRA adapters with pipeline parallelism on **a single machine with four RTX A6000 48 GB cards over PCIe 4.0 x16** and 256 GB of RAM, with Llama2-7B/13B on GSM8K, against HF PEFT, 1F1B, Megatron tensor parallelism and FSDP, cutting task completion time 30-45% ([mLoRA](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol18/p1948-tang.pdf)). Its code URL was not verified. **LoRAFusion** (EuroSys'26, header and DOI only) already beats it, reports up to 1.96x over Megatron-LM, includes results on **four L40S 48 GB cards over PCIe**, and ships code ([LoRAFusion](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.00206v1), [code](https://github.com/CentML/lorafusion)). It leaves one item: "Tensor parallelism is not evaluated due to the lack of efficient support in existing LoRA frameworks." With LobRA, tLoRA and PLoRA alongside, the line is crowded ([LobRA](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.01193v1), [tLoRA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07263), [PLoRA](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.02932)).

Co-serving inference with fine-tuning is thinner. **FlexLLM** (NSDI'26) shares a model between inference and PEFT at token granularity on four-A100-80GB nodes with 8B to 32B models, ships an artifact, and names "extending our implementation beyond offline finetuning scenarios where sequences are processed individually (batch size 1)" as open ([FlexLLM](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.18789v3)). **Loquetier** (NeurIPS'25) ran its inference-only experiments "on a server with 4 NVIDIA A6000 48G GPUs" and its fine-tuning ones on H800s, beats FlexLLM and S-LoRA with PEFT by up to 3.0x on inference, and ships code ([Loquetier](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/hash/5ebc22174f6dff6fa7c2447d0dfa31ea-Abstract-Conference.html), [code](https://github.com/NJUDeepEngine/Loquetier)). Memory-efficient training offers two more fits without follow-ups found: the ATC'25 Jenga for long-context fine-tuning, with a single A40 48 GB among its platforms and artifact badges (distinct from the SOSP'25 allocator of the same name), and ProTrain (MLSys'26) on four RTX 3090s without code ([Jenga ATC'25](https://www.usenix.org/conference/atc25/presentation/wang-tuowei), [ProTrain](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08334)). These are training-systems problems, and the student's vLLM skills do not transfer to them.

### Offline inference papers each built their own workload

**BatchLLM** (MLSys'26 Industry) is built on vLLM v0.6.4, shares prefixes globally across a batch, runs Llama-3.1-8B and Qwen-2.5-14B on one A100 80 GB, and gains 1.3x to 10.8x over vLLM and SGLang ([BatchLLM](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.03594v3), [code](https://github.com/microsoft/MixLLM/tree/batchllm_vllm_064)). Its workloads are synthetic plus a private industry set. It leaves a hybrid of streaming and batch serving open: "We leave the implementation of such a hybrid system for future work." **ConServe** (ICML'26) is that hybrid in part: online and offline co-serving on vLLM 0.8.4 with chunked prefill, BurstGPT online and LongBench document tasks offline, 2.2x offline throughput against preemptive Sarathi and DistServe variants, on one H100 with 8B and 14B models; the notes found no code link ([ConServe](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.01228)). **BlendServe** (ASPLOS'26, header and DOI) reorders offline requests for resource overlap on SGLang with NanoFlow, ships a Docker artifact, and states both that "predicting output length may not always be accurate" and that there is "no open-sourced trace available for offline batch inference" ([BlendServe](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.16102v2)). The relational-analytics paper at MLSys'25 built its own benchmark "Due to the lack of standard workloads in this area" on a single L4 with vLLM ([LLM queries in relational analytics](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.05821)). **BatchGen** (OSDI'26) defines batch completion time on 8 to 128 H20/H200 cards with models from 47B to 671B, only promises code, and includes VeRL RL rollouts among its workloads, which touches dropped direction D4 ([BatchGen](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.21712)).

Three of these papers say independently that no shared offline workload exists. That is the opening: the mechanisms are on the student's engine and fit one or two cards, and the missing piece is a public, replayable batch workload. Evaluation cost, by contrast, is treated as statistics: tinyBenchmarks (ICML'24) shows 100 examples per scenario estimate scores within about 2% and runs on a CPU ([tinyBenchmarks](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.14992)). The notes found no top-venue paper that benchmarks an end-to-end distillation or synthetic-data pipeline as a system, which may be a search gap.

## The shortlist: three setups meet nearly every criterion, five miss one

The four selection criteria are: (a) the published testbed matches or nearly matches one node with about four idle 48 GB cards; (b) code and workloads are public; (c) the authors state open problems themselves; (d) few follow-ups have been found. No catalogued setup is clean on all four with nothing left to verify. The table ranks the eight that come closest, and the paragraphs after it give the reasoning and the setups that were considered and rejected.

### Why the top three lead

**SpecReason** is first because it is the only catalogued paper whose stated testbed is literally the student's: two A6000-48GB cards, vLLM, prefix caching, tensor-parallel two. Its stated future work, finer-grained verification through log-probability confidence or dynamic thresholds, is a modest method extension. The larger opening is the one the paper does not evaluate: its two-model, prefill-scored loop under concurrent load, where the base model's scoring passes and the small model's generation compete for the same cards and the same prefix cache. That turns a model-level result into a scheduling result on the engine the student knows. It misses criterion (d) in part, with two follow-ups found and the wider reasoning-efficiency area busy. It does not fall in dropped direction D1, because it changes which model produces tokens, not which KV pages are attended.

**DiffKV** is second because it meets all four criteria on paper and is the only KV-cache system in the catalogue evaluated on 48 GB cards inside vLLM. The risk is proximity to dropped direction D1. The student's negative result concerned selecting KV pages at decode time for long reasoning outputs; DiffKV includes QwQ-32B and R1 distillations on AIME24 and uses Quest as a baseline, so part of its evaluation covers that same ground. The student should reproduce it knowing that the reasoning-model rows may confirm the earlier negative finding, and that such a confirmation, set against DiffKV's published 1.9x to 5.4x, would be a result worth reporting. The parts outside D1 are the memory manager and the mixed-precision policy.

**The IISWC'26 agentic workload study** is third because it hands over a measured, unserved workload on the student's engine at the student's model size. Its central finding, a throughput drop of up to 66% when agent contexts thrash the prefix cache, is a KV-cache and scheduling problem with no published system response and no follow-up found. It connects directly to the open problem that five independent sources state, state held on the GPU while agents wait on tools. Criterion (a) is a near miss, and the miss helps: 48 GB cards have less KV headroom than H100 NVL cards, so thrashing should begin at lower concurrency and be easier to provoke on the node. The weak points are a venue that is author-stated, a characterization paper as the base, and an agent harness driven by Claude Code, which a reproduction would also need.

### Why the next five trail

**LMetric** has the cleanest idea and the best traces but was run on sixteen 96 GB cards. Its design of one vLLM instance per GPU reduces to four instances, and nobody has shown whether a routing effect measured across sixteen instances remains visible across four. The student has already replayed the Mooncake traces for dropped direction D2 and found little to gain from caching hybrid-model state; LMetric uses the same trace for a different question, where to send a request, and with dense models. **JITServe** is the most current single-engine scheduler with code and three stated gaps, and three of its four models fit; it sits in a line where every paper benchmarks against the last, so a follow-up must beat it on its own workloads. **SPEX** fits for its small models, has code and no successors, and states a concrete weakness on skewed trees; it is built on SGLang, and its 30B and 34B results were on A100s in unstated numbers. **BatchLLM** is on vLLM, fits one card, and names the hybrid streaming-plus-batch system as future work; ConServe has since covered part of that without releasing code, and the missing public workload means the student would have to build the benchmark first. That benchmark would itself be a contribution, since three papers say none exists. **Nixie** has the fullest artifact and three stated openings with no successors, and it is the furthest from the student's stack: Ollama, llama.cpp and SGLang on consumer cards, with driver-level mechanisms.

### Setups that match the hardware and were rejected

Hardware fit is common in the catalogue and is not sufficient. **Parrot** (four A6000s) and **mLoRA** (four A6000s on PCIe 4.0) are exact matches in crowded lines: seven systems followed Autellix in program-aware scheduling, and multi-LoRA training has at least five systems in two years. **Fiddler** matches an RTX 6000 Ada exactly, ships code, and states open problems, but handles few concurrent queries and has at least four follow-ups. **CacheBlend** (two A40s) and **ServerlessLLM** (A40 servers) are both standard baselines in later papers. **Chameleon** (one A40) and **METIS** (two A40s) have no code. **Loquetier** ran its inference half on four A6000s with code, but its contribution is on the fine-tuning side, which needed H800s. **Rethinking KV Cache Compression** ran mostly on A6000s with code and is best used as the critical companion to DiffKV, not as a base of its own. **VTC**, **Pie** and **The Cost of Dynamic Reasoning** are sound second-tier options: each fits, ships code and states open problems, the notes found no crowd of successors for any of them, and each sits on an engine other than a current vLLM (S-LoRA/LightLLM, Pie's own runtime, and vLLM 0.6.6 respectively).

## What the notes cannot establish

Every fit verdict is an assessment from reported hardware. None of these systems was installed or run for this report. An L40 or L40S is not an A6000: memory matches, compute does not, and any throughput figure will shift. The node's host DRAM, NVMe bandwidth and CPU instruction set are unknown here, which leaves the offload papers (Strata, Fiddler, PowerInfer, Kairox, NEO) undecided regardless of GPU fit.

Several venues rest on the paper's own header or the authors' statement and were not checked against a program: EuroSys'26 for KunServe, LoRAFusion, STAlloc and SkyWalker (the EuroSys'26 accepted-papers page returned an error); ASPLOS'26 for BlendServe; HPCA'26 for PASCAL and The Cost of Dynamic Reasoning; IISWC'26 for the agentic workload study; MLSys'26 for TeleRAG; EuroSys'25 for CacheBlend. DiffKV's SOSP'25 listing was matched by title, with facts taken from the arXiv version. Learning to Rank and Chameleon were confirmed at search level only.

Follow-up counts are lower bounds. No citation-graph search was run; the counts come from papers that happened to appear in the same survey and from targeted searches, one of which (for Hogwild!) was rate-limited. "None found" for a 2026 paper says little, and before committing to any shortlisted setup the student should run a citation search on it. "No code link found" likewise does not prove that no code exists. In the serving area, ASPLOS'25, MLSys'25, MLSys'26, ISCA, MICRO and ATC'26 were not surveyed systematically. The agent and reasoning notes were extracted from the first part of each arXiv HTML page, and a few numbers differ between abstract and body.

The last unverified item is engine age. The shortlisted vLLM-based systems pin old releases: SpecReason used 0.8.2, BatchLLM 0.6.4, ConServe 0.8.4, and the notes do not record DiffKV's base. The student profiles vLLM 0.32. Reproduction therefore means either running the pinned version, where the baseline is weaker than today's, or rebasing, where the published gain may already be partly absorbed by upstream. Either outcome is informative, and the choice should be made deliberately before any numbers are compared with the paper.

## Conclusion

The catalogue's most useful pattern is where the open setups sit. Lines defined by a mechanism inside one engine (chunked prefill, disaggregation, tiered KV offload, program-aware queues, multi-LoRA training) fill within a year, and a matching testbed does not protect a newcomer, as Parrot and mLoRA show. The setups that remain open are at a boundary: a model-level technique that has only been measured one request at a time (SpecReason, SPEX, Hogwild!), or a workload that has been characterized and not yet served (agent sessions that thrash the prefix cache, offline batches with no public trace). The student's dropped directions point the same way. Decode at 89-95% of the hardware limit and a 2.6-5.5% ceiling on hybrid-model caching both say that the engine's inner loop is close to done on this hardware; the remaining gains come from doing less work per request (speculating, reusing, routing), and those are decided above the kernel.

The recommended order is to reproduce SpecReason first, because it costs the least: the hardware is identical, two of the four cards stay free, and success or failure shows up quickly. In parallel, the student should run a citation search on DiffKV and the IISWC'26 study to test the "no follow-ups" finding, and check the node's host memory and storage. If SpecReason reproduces, the paper is its behaviour under concurrent load on current vLLM. If the agentic thrashing result reproduces on 48 GB cards, it combines with LMetric's routing rule and the Bailian and Mooncake traces into a second, larger project: KV-aware admission and routing for agent sessions on a four-instance node, a problem that five sources name and no catalogued system solves.
