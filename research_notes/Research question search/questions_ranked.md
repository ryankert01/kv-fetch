## 1. Q03 (mean 4.25, min 4; S4 F4 G5 R4)

**Question.** At what server load does sampling k reasoning chains and taking the first finishers stop beating one long chain in accuracy per GPU-second, and does that crossover sit at the batch occupancy where the engine turns from memory-bound to compute-bound?

- Lenses: contradictions; tension type: Three papers give three different rules (short parallel chains win; one long chain wins; it depends on latency versus tokens); the third names the regime variable but does not measure it
- Why unknown: The parallel-wins results are measured with the GPU serving one question at a time, where extra branches use idle compute. The latency-aware paper says the result flips on a loaded server but only sketches this in an appendix. No paper measures where the flip happens, or which accuracy claim holds when the unit is GPU-seconds under load.
- If yes: If the crossover tracks the memory-bound to compute-bound boundary: one measured engine curve (throughput versus batch occupancy) predicts the right thinking strategy at any load, which points to choosing between branching and continuing based on load.
- If no: If it does not: something else sets the crossover (variance of chain length, early-stop savings, KV memory per branch), and the roofline argument used by both the test-time-scaling and speculative-decoding papers is not sufficient.
- First experiment: DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-14B and Qwen3-14B (thinking mode), one 48 GB card each, vLLM 0.32. Data: AIME 2024 + 2025 and HMMT Feb 2025 (the first paper's sets) plus MATH-500 for statistical power. Record 20 sampled chains per question once with per-token timestamps. Then run each strategy (one chain with a long budget; majority@k; short-1@k; short-3@k; k in 2, 4, 8, 16) while the server holds a fixed number of other active sequences (0, 8, 32, 64, 128). Measured numbers: accuracy at equal wall-clock and at equal GPU-seconds per question, and the occupancy at which the parallel strategy's latency advantage reaches zero.
- Who cares: Serving teams that price reasoning effort, and authors of test-time-scaling papers who report token counts or single-request latency.
- Closest existing answer: The latency-aware test-time-scaling paper (appendix A.4) states the regime change without measuring it. SART (arXiv 2505.13326) and a parallel MCTS serving paper (arXiv 2604.00510) handle branching under load but, per search summaries only, do not compare against one long chain at equal GPU-seconds.
- Overlap with dropped directions: none (it uses the D3 throughput-versus-batch curves as an input)
- Evidence:
  - Within a question, shorter chains are more often correct, and taking the first finishers among k parallel samples beats majority voting while being faster. (Don't Overthink it. Preferring Shorter Thinking Chains for Improved LLM Reasoning, arXiv 2025, venue not confirmed; https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.17813): shorter reasoning chains within individual questions are significantly more likely to yield correct answers—up to 34.5% more accurate than the longest chain sampled for the same question.
  - The opposite rule: one long chain can beat many short ones, including on AIME with s1-32B. (Let Me Think! A Long Chain-of-Thought Can Be Worth Exponentially Many Short Ones, NeurIPS 2025; https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.21825): We also conducted experiments with the s1-32B model (Muennighoff et al., 2025) on the AIME2024 dataset. The results show that sequential scaling can not be efficiently replaced by parallel scaling for this mathematical task
  - Sequential is better per token but parallel is better per second, and the authors concede this holds only when the server is not busy. (Faster and Better LLMs via Latency-Aware Test-Time Scaling, Findings of EMNLP 2025 (venue from search); https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.19634): although sequential scaling has a better token efficiency, achieving a similar performance with 9× fewer tokens compared to parallel scaling, it turns out that parallel scaling achieves 1.6× lower latency. ... For large-scale servers, which deals with hundreds or thousands of requests at one time, the main bottleneck of inference is computation, and the budget of inference can be measured by #tokens. (Second sentence is from the Limitations section.)
  - A later paper reports sequential scaling beating parallel self-consistency at matched compute in most configurations. (The Sequential Edge: Inverse-Entropy Voting Beats Parallel Self-Consistency at Matched Compute, arXiv 2511.02309, 2025; https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.02309) [unverified]: 95.6% of configurations (search summary only; paper not opened)
- Judges: surprise: Published papers disagree on parallel versus sequential thinking and none locates the load crossover; either a roofline-tracking crossover or a different driver changes how test-time strategy is chosen under load. / feasible: 14B reasoning models fit on one card and chains can be recorded once and replayed, but only about 15 GB of KV remains, so the 64-128 background-sequence occupancy points need a smaller model or simulation. / generative: Either answer leads to a build: a load-aware controller that switches between branching and continuing, or a named non-roofline factor (length variance, per-branch KV) to design around, and neither is tied to one model or engine. / reviewer: Parallel versus sequential test-time scaling is an active contradiction and the latency-aware TTS paper itself concedes the result flips under load without measuring it; I know of no paper locating the crossover in GPU-seconds on a loaded engine.

## 2. Q05 (mean 4.25, min 4; S4 F4 G5 R4)

**Question.** Which lossy inference techniques (weight quantization, KV-cache quantization, KV eviction or page selection) make a reasoning model think longer, by enough to cancel the per-token savings, and is the length change set by the kind of approximation, the model or problem difficulty, or predicted for every method by one measurable property of the perturbation (such as the shift in next-token distribution from the full-cache run), so that a per-token saving can be converted to an end-to-end saving in advance?

- Lenses: contradictions, regime_shifts, composition; tension type: Direct three-sided contradiction between papers on which compression family lengthens outputs
- Why unknown: Each paper uses different models, tasks, quantizers and budgets. None puts eviction, quantization and a matched random perturbation on one axis. Candidate explaining variables nobody has separated: the quantizer (KIVI versus QuaRot), the size of the accuracy loss, the task (code, maths, chat), and whether the damage lands on the tokens the model uses to decide it is done.
- If yes: If one quantity predicts lengthening: the length cost can be forecast offline from a cheap divergence measure, the papers' disagreement reduces to different operating points, and reported throughput gains can be corrected for length.
- If no: If it depends on the method: there is a specific mechanism (for example eviction removing an earlier result the model checks against, or quantization noise moving the end-of-thinking decision), which says which part of the cache has to be protected.
- First experiment: DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-14B and GPT-OSS-20B (ThinKV's model), HF Transformers with the public R-KV code and NVIDIA's kvpress, one 48 GB card each. MATH-500 plus AIME 2024/2025, 8 samples per question. Conditions: full cache; KIVI at 4 and 2 bits; QuaRot KV4; R-KV and H2O at three budgets; Gaussian noise added to cached keys and values, scaled to match each method's divergence. Measured numbers: mean generated tokens relative to full cache, accuracy, and KL between compressed and full next-token distributions on teacher-forced full-cache traces. The test is whether length ratio versus KL falls on one curve across methods.
- Who cares: Authors of KV-compression papers that report tokens/s without output length, and serving teams deciding whether FP8 or 4-bit KV is free for reasoning models.
- Closest existing answer: ThinKV (ICLR 2026) asserts the quantization-versus-eviction split from one figure on one model; "Hold Onto That Thought" reports the opposite for eviction. Neither tests a common variable or a matched-noise control. | ThinKV's length analysis (quantization versus eviction on one model) and the quantized-reasoning study (lengths match except at aggressive bit widths). They conflict with each other and cover neither hybrids nor selection methods. | arXiv 2606.00206 gives a mechanism and a mitigation for weight quantization only, with no KV methods and no throughput. arXiv 2504.04823 reports lengths for weight, activation and KV quantization without end-to-end time. The MLSys 2025 study covers chat models with a 1024-token cap. ThinKV contrasts quantization and eviction in a single sentence.
- Overlap with dropped directions: D1, substantial: same setting (KV subset or compression during long reasoning outputs). The difference is the dependent variable, output length and its cause, not selection quality. If D1 compared methods without logging output length, this is a gap in D1's own measurement. | D1. It reuses the D1 harness and would explain D1's loss mode (truncation at the cap). It does not change D1's margin over Quest. | D1 in part (KV eviction for long reasoning outputs is one arm of the experiment; the question is about output length, not page selection quality)
- Evidence:
  - ThinKV: quantization lengthens reasoning outputs, eviction does not. (ThinKV: Thought-Adaptive KV Cache Compression for Efficient Reasoning Models, ICLR 2026 (oral); https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.01290): Unlike quantization, eviction does not cause an increase in generation length (preceded by: 'in LRMs we find that aggressive quantization inflates L_gen, eroding memory savings and simultaneously degrading accuracy', where L_gen is a math symbol; KIVI quantizer, R-KV eviction, GPT-OSS-20B on LiveCodeBench)
  - The opposite for eviction: low budgets produce longer traces, and every compressed setting has lower end-to-end tokens/s than the full cache. (Hold Onto That Thought: Assessing KV Cache Compression On Reasoning, arXiv 2512.12008, 2025 (preprint; track of its NeurIPS 2025 virtual page not confirmed); https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.12008): We also find that eviction strategies at low budgets can produce longer reasoning traces, revealing a tradeoff between cache size and inference costs. (Table 5, GSM8K end-to-end tokens/s: Full 30.71; H2O 23.41 to 25.81; R-KV 27.72 to 30.04.)
  - The opposite for quantization: 4-bit KV quantization does not lengthen reasoning outputs; only more aggressive settings do. (Quantization Hurts Reasoning? An Empirical Study on Quantized Reasoning Models, arXiv 2504.04823, 2025, venue not confirmed; https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.04823): for various quantization configurations (e.g., W4G128, W8A8KV8, and KV4), the generated output lengths closely align with those of the BF16 models. However, under more aggressive low-bit quantization (e.g., W3G128 and W4A4KV4), LLMs tend to produce longer outputs.
  - On a chat model, both quantization and eviction lengthen outputs enough to cancel throughput gains. (Rethinking Key-Value Cache Compression Techniques for Large Language Model Serving, MLSys 2025; https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.24000): KV cache compression leans toward generating lengthy responses. Specifically, more than 20% of the samples show at least a 1.5× increase in response length.
  - R-KV's headline throughput comes from fitting larger batches, not from faster single requests. (R-KV: Redundancy-aware KV Cache Compression for Reasoning Models, NeurIPS 2025; https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.24133): The primary throughput improvement from R-KV stems from enabling significantly larger inference batch sizes due to KV cache compression. (Headline: 6.6× throughput at 10% budget, 16K sequence length.)
  - On chat data, KV compression lengthens responses for a sizeable share of samples. (Rethinking Key-Value Cache Compression Techniques for Large Language Model Serving, MLSys 2025; https://arxiv.org/html/2503.24000): KV cache compression leans toward generating lengthy responses. Specifically, more than 20% of the samples show at least a 1.5× increase in response length.
  - On a reasoning model, quantization inflates length strongly and eviction does not. (ThinKV, ICLR 2026 (Oral); https://arxiv.org/html/2510.01290): our R1-Llama-8B results show that pure quantization can inflate generation length by up to 5.1×. In contrast, eviction-based approaches do not induce such drastic inflation. ... substantial generation-length inflation negates most of the compression gains, yielding only a modest 1.1× improvement in throughput
  - A separate study of quantized reasoning models finds no length increase except at aggressive bit widths. (Quantization Hurts Reasoning? An Empirical Study on Quantized Reasoning Models, arXiv 2504.04823 (venue not confirmed); https://arxiv.org/html/2504.04823): Contrary to expectations, quantized models do not exhibit increased output lengths. ... under more aggressive low-bit quantization (e.g., W3G128 and W4A4KV4), LLMs tend to produce longer outputs.
  - Headline throughput for reasoning-model KV compression is stated at a fixed sequence length. (R-KV, NeurIPS 2025; https://arxiv.org/html/2505.24133): at a sequence length of 16K, R-KV achieves up to 9× larger batch sizes and over 6.6× higher throughput under a 10% compression ratio
  - The student's accuracy losses under page selection were mostly runs that hit the output cap. (Project notes, E3 pilot and hybrid H3 (memory file kv-fetch-phase0.md); /home/ryankert_l/.claude/projects/-home-ryankert-l-kv-fetch-kv-fetch/memory/kv-fetch-phase0.md): Losses are mostly truncation at 16K (not verbatim loops, except SnapKV); hybrid H3: losses mostly 32K truncation
  - An empirical study of quantized reasoning models states in its abstract that output length does not grow, while its own text reports longer outputs at aggressive bit widths. (Quantization Hurts Reasoning? An Empirical Study on Quantized Reasoning Models (Liu et al.; arXiv preprint 2504.04823, 2025); https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.04823): "Contrary to expectations, quantized models do not exhibit increased output lengths."; "under more aggressive low-bit quantization (e.g., W3G128 and W4A4KV4), LLMs tend to produce longer outputs."
  - A later paper reports a large length increase under weight quantization together with an accuracy drop, and a negative rank correlation between accuracy and length across 28 model-quantization pairs. A search result lists it as a NeurIPS 2026 oral; the venue page was not opened. It covers weight quantization only and reports no throughput. (Quantized Reasoning Models Think They Need to Think Longer, but They Do Not (Lotfi, Kirichenko, Li, Liu; arXiv 2606.00206, 2026); https://arxiv.org/html/2606.00206): "3-bit AWQ reduces accuracy from 85.6% to 47.0%" while "increasing the average CoT from 5.2K to 23.4K tokens" (MATH-500, R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B); Spearman rho = -0.73
  - ThinKV says quantization inflates length but eviction does not. The sentence was read; which method and bit width produce 5.1x was not confirmed. (ThinKV: Thought-Adaptive KV Cache Compression for Efficient Reasoning Models (ICLR 2026); https://arxiv.org/html/2510.01290): "pure quantization can inflate generation length by up to 5.1×"; "eviction-based approaches do not induce such drastic inflation."
  - On chat models with a 1024-token cap, both quantization and eviction lengthen responses by similar factors, and the longer responses can cancel the throughput gain. (Rethinking Key-Value Cache Compression Techniques for Large Language Model Serving (MLSys 2025); https://arxiv.org/html/2503.24000): Table 4 (LLaMA-7B) length increase: 1.69x (KIVI-4), 1.70x (GEAR-4), 1.55x (H2O-512), 1.76x (StreamingLLM-512); "the lengthier responses produced by these methods can outweigh the throughput speedup benefits"
  - R-KV reports throughput gains for reasoning models from larger batches but no change in per-response length. (R-KV: Redundancy-aware KV Cache Compression for Reasoning Models (NeurIPS 2025); https://arxiv.org/html/2505.24133): "at a sequence length of 16K, R-KV achieves up to 9× larger batch sizes and over 6.6× higher throughput"
- Judges: surprise: Papers directly contradict each other on which approximations lengthen reasoning; one predictive curve or a method-specific mechanism would each change how compression savings are reported. / feasible: Public R-KV and kvpress code with 14B-20B models fits one card each, but about 12 conditions at 8 samples in slow HF Transformers generation needs a MATH-500 subset, and the matched-noise and QuaRot arms need custom plumbing. / generative: A single predictor gives an offline way to convert per-token savings into end-to-end savings for any lossy method, and a method-specific answer names which part of the cache must be protected; both generalize across models and engines. / reviewer: ThinKV, Hold Onto That Thought, the quantized-reasoning study and MLSys'25 visibly contradict each other on length inflation and nobody has put the methods on one axis with a matched-noise control; arXiv 2606.00206 answers only the weight-quantization part.

## 3. Q24 (mean 4.25, min 4; S5 F4 G4 R4)

**Question.** For open reasoning models running multi-turn tool-using agent tasks, does keeping earlier turns' thinking in the context (versus only the latest turn's thinking or none) raise task success enough to pay for the longer context in GPU time per solved task, or does it act as context pollution, and does the sign depend on whether the model was trained to see its own reasoning?

- Lenses: regime_shifts, practitioner_disputes; tension type: Vendor guidance reversal (model cards) plus conflicting paper results; agent harnesses disagree on the default.
- Why unknown: The vendor reversed its guidance between Qwen3 and Qwen3.6 without publishing an ablation. One paper finds a gain from keeping reasoning on a model told to strip it; another finds assistant history is mostly dispensable in chat. No source found measures accuracy and compute together (context growth, cache hits, reasoning tokens saved) with the flag on and off on a model trained to preserve, in a tool loop.
- If yes: Reasoning traces act as working memory. The follow-up is which parts carry the benefit and whether a compressed form keeps it, and context budgets for agent serving need to count them.
- If no: Stripping is right even for models trained to preserve, and the serving problem becomes how to strip without paying a full re-prefill (on hybrid models this needs a state checkpoint at the strip point).
- First experiment: A 2x2 on one or two 48 GB GPUs: Qwen3.6-27B-FP8 (trained to preserve) and Qwen3-8B (told to strip), each with and without prior-turn reasoning in history, same chat template otherwise, on BFCL multi-turn plus about 50 multi-step tasks from a public agent benchmark that runs locally. Number measured: task accuracy, generated reasoning tokens per task, prefill tokens actually computed (cache misses) per task, and GPU-seconds per solved task.
- Who cares: Agent harness authors (opencode, hermes-agent and koboldcpp all changed behaviour on this in 2026), serving teams sizing context and cache, and the model vendor.
- Closest existing answer: SWE-AGILE (one model, one benchmark, accuracy only) and the audio paper's ablation (different model, tau2-bench). No controlled study across policies that reports accuracy together with serving cost was found. | arXiv 2606.00135: accuracy on BFCL multi-turn for Qwen3 only; no compute accounting, no trained-to-preserve model, no long-horizon loop. arXiv 2602.24287 covers chat conversations and assistant turns in general, not reasoning traces in tool loops. No head-to-head ablation of preserve_thinking on/off was found by search.
- Overlap with dropped directions: D2, partly. If dropping thinking wins, the strip point is where a hybrid needs a state checkpoint, the same mechanism as D2 on a workload different from the Mooncake traces. Whether the ceiling is larger than D2's 2.6-5.5% is not known. | D2: stripping reasoning rewrites history and breaks the prefix cache, and on hybrids the remedy is a state checkpoint at the strip point. The question itself is about accuracy versus cost, not cache policy.
- Evidence:
  - The vendor says retention helps agents, reduces tokens and improves cache use, with no numbers; the default keeps only the latest turn's thinking. (Qwen3.6-27B model card (Hugging Face README); https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.6-27B): By default, only the thinking blocks generated in handling the latest user message is retained ... This capability is particularly beneficial for agent scenarios, where maintaining full reasoning context can enhance decision consistency and, in many cases, reduce overall token consumption by minimizing redundant reasoning. Additionally, it can improve KV cache utilization, optimizing inference efficiency in both thinking and non-thinking modes.
  - On Qwen3-8B and SWE-Bench-Verified, retaining full reasoning history lowered the resolve rate. (SWE-AGILE, arXiv 2604.11716 (venue not confirmed); https://arxiv.org/html/2604.11716): Retaining full reasoning history (Interleaved Thinking) significantly degrades performance (12.42%) compared to discarding it (Current-Step Thinking)(15.83%), despite the richer context. Additionally, we hypothesize that this degradation may also be partially related to the post-training process of Qwen3 models.
  - On tau2-bench with another model, dropping all thinking cost 3-5 points, and full retention matched latest-turn retention at longer contexts. (Unified Audio Intelligence Without Regressing on Text Intelligence, arXiv 2607.05196 (venue not confirmed); https://arxiv.org/html/2607.05196): this evaluation setup consistently reduces scores by 3–5 points relative to latest-turn thought retention. ... We also tested a full thought retention policy ... but found it gives similar accuracy to latest-turn thought retention while incurring substantially longer contexts.
  - Thinking is a large share of agent output tokens, so the retention policy changes context growth by a large factor. (Agentic AI Workload Characteristics, IISWC 2026 (venue as stated by authors); https://arxiv.org/html/2605.26297): Gemma Thinking spends 45.8–67.6% of its output on thinking across workloads, while Qwen Thinking spends 29.0–40.7%
  - Vendor model card: Qwen3 guidance is to strip reasoning from history. (Qwen3-8B model card (vendor documentation, Hugging Face, 2025); https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3-8B): **No Thinking Content in History**: In multi-turn conversations, the historical model output should only include the final output part and does not need to include the thinking content.
  - Vendor model card: Qwen3.6 reverses this, claiming accuracy, token and cache benefits from keeping reasoning; no numbers are given. (Qwen3.6-27B model card (vendor documentation, Hugging Face, 2026); https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.6-27B): This capability is particularly beneficial for agent scenarios, where maintaining full reasoning context can enhance decision consistency and, in many cases, reduce overall token consumption by minimizing redundant reasoning. Additionally, it can improve KV cache utilization, optimizing inference efficiency in both thinking and non-thinking modes.
  - Paper: retaining thinking history improves BFCL multi-turn accuracy on Qwen3, a model whose own card says to strip it. (On Effectiveness and Efficiency of Agentic Tool-calling and RL Training (arXiv preprint 2606.00135, 2026); https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.00135): Takeaway 3.3 Retaining thinking history improves by ∼2–5% on BFCL multi-turn for reasoning models.
  - Paper: in chat, dropping or summarising prior assistant turns often matches full history at far less context, and carried-over reasoning is named as a pollution source. (Do LLMs Benefit From Their Own Words? (arXiv preprint 2602.24287, Feb 2026); https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.24287): replacing all prior assistant turns with one-sentence summaries or keeping only the most recent user–assistant exchange often matches storing full context in performance while using roughly 8× less context
  - PR thread: whether reasoning is passed back also decides whether the prefix cache hits at all in a real harness. (opencode PR #28352, fix(provider): skip empty reasoning_content to preserve KV cache hits (GitHub PR thread, closed, 2026); https://github.com/anomalyco/opencode/pull/28352): WITH reasoning_content → 97.2% cache hit, WITHOUT → 0% cache hit
  - Issue thread: another harness silently dropped prior reasoning even when the server was set to preserve it. (hermes-agent issue #56004 (GitHub issue thread, closed, 2026); https://github.com/NousResearch/hermes-agent/issues/56004): the model's chain-of-thought from earlier turns is never fed back to it on subsequent turns, even when the server is configured to preserve it.
  - Paper reported to show that selectively omitting thoughts and observations cuts tokens without hurting accuracy; not opened. (Agent-Omit (arXiv 2602.04284; ICML 2026 per search summary); https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.04284) [unverified]: not opened; no quote taken
- Judges: surprise: The vendor reversed its guidance and papers report opposite signs; either answer changes agent context budgets or how reasoning is stripped, so informed people would bet differently. / feasible: A 2x2 on Qwen3.6-27B-FP8 and Qwen3-8B with BFCL multi-turn and about 50 local agent tasks fits on one or two cards, though the two models differ in size as well as training and small task sets give wide accuracy intervals. / generative: A 'yes' leads to compressed reasoning as working memory and a 'no' leads to cheap strip mechanisms, but the sign may depend on each model's training and the 'no' branch returns to D2 checkpoints. / reviewer: The vendor reversed its guidance without an ablation and SWE-AGILE, arXiv 2606.00135 and the tau2 ablation disagree in sign, so a controlled accuracy-plus-serving-cost study of thinking retention is visibly wanted; partly answered piecemeal on accuracy alone.

## 4. Q11 (mean 4.25, min 3; S4 F5 G3 R5)

**Question.** How much of the latency and throughput gain that length-aware and program-aware LLM schedulers (SJF by predicted length, MLFQ, Autellix-style program priority) show under open-loop Poisson arrivals survives when the same engine is loaded by a fixed population of closed-loop multi-turn agent sessions, where each next request is issued only after the previous response and tool call return, or does request ordering stop mattering while memory admission sets throughput?

- Lenses: assumptions, regime_shifts; tension type: Shared untested assumption (open-loop Poisson arrivals, independent requests) versus a classic queueing result and production agent traces that put agent sessions on the closed-loop side
- Why unknown: The Schroeder result is for classic single-server queues. An LLM engine with continuous batching runs all admitted requests together and queues only when KV memory or the batch limit is reached, so neither the open nor the closed prediction transfers directly. Autellix reports gains in a partly-open setup with about 160 calls per program, which the classic result would not predict. I found no LLM scheduling paper that reports its gain under both loop types on the same request population.
- If yes: If most of the gain survives closed-loop load, something specific to batched LLM engines (memory-driven preemption, cache thrashing, long programs holding KV memory) makes ordering matter where classic theory says it should not. Isolating that mechanism would say where program-aware scheduling earns its gain.
- If no: If the gain mostly disappears, request ordering is a second-order knob for agent and rollout serving and the first-order knobs are admission and memory control. Speedups published under Poisson arrivals would need a loop-type caveat, and the number of calls per session at which the gain halves becomes a reporting requirement for scheduler papers.
- First experiment: vLLM with Qwen3-8B or Qwen3.5-9B on one 48 GB GPU. Policies: FCFS, oracle SJF per call, oracle shortest-remaining-program. Loads built from the same request population (calls per session, token counts and tool durations from the public GitHub Copilot coding-agent dataset in AzurePublicDataset): (a) open-loop Poisson per call at 70-95% of capacity, (b) closed loop with N concurrent sessions, (c) partly-open with Poisson session arrivals and k calls per session for k in {1, 5, 15, 100}. Measure the ratio of mean and P99 session completion time, FCFS over SJF, as a function of loop type and k, and report the k at which the gain halves.
- Who cares: Authors and reviewers of LLM scheduling papers (OSDI/NSDI/NeurIPS line: Sarathi-Serve, Learning-to-Rank, Autellix), operators of agent and RL-rollout serving, and benchmark builders who must choose open-loop or closed-loop replay.
- Closest existing answer: Schroeder et al., NSDI 2006, answers it for classic queues, not for continuous-batching LLM engines. Human-less LLM Serving (arXiv 2606.20577) runs a closed-loop agent workload but tests chunk size and SLO settings, not ordering policies. Not opened: Mitzenmacher and Shahout, "Queueing, Predictions, and Large Language Models: Challenges and Open Problems", which may discuss closed systems. None found that compares scheduler gains across loop types on an LLM engine. | Schroeder et al. NSDI 2006 answers it for general systems without memory state. Autellix states the feedback effect but evaluates with Poisson program arrivals. A NeurIPS 2026 ML-for-Systems workshop poster, 'From Conversational Chats to Agents: Workload-Dependent Bottleneck Shifts in LLM Serving' (openreview.net/forum?id=mTDnSps98P, seen only in a search summary, not opened), reports that scheduling cannot compensate for exhausted GPU capacity; the summary shows no open-versus-closed comparison. No direct answer found in LLM serving after two searches.
- Overlap with dropped directions: D4. RL rollouts are a closed-loop workload, and the D4 result (watermark setting alone within 7% of a perfect schedule) is one data point consistent with the closed-system prediction. This card asks whether that holds in general, so part of the answer may already be in the student's D4 data. | D4. The RL rollout workload was closed-loop and its result (watermark alone within 6.4-6.8% of perfect packing) is one data point for the 'gains vanish' answer. A new scheduler here should expect the same small headroom D4 found; what remains open is the measurement across arrival models and against published claims.
- Evidence:
  - Sarathi-Serve evaluates with open-loop Poisson arrivals. (Taming Throughput-Latency Tradeoff in LLM Inference with Sarathi-Serve, OSDI 2024; https://arxiv.org/html/2403.02310): "The request arrival times are generated using Poisson distribution."
  - DistServe evaluates with open-loop Poisson arrivals. (DistServe: Disaggregating Prefill and Decoding for Goodput-optimized LLM Serving, OSDI 2024; https://arxiv.org/html/2401.09670): "generate request arrival times using Poisson distribution with different request rates"
  - Autellix lets whole programs arrive by a Poisson process; its programs have many calls each, and a gap to SRPT remains. ## 32. Q25 (mean 2.5, min 2; S2 F3 G3 R2)

**Question.** Is the thinking-token KV that can be evicted within a turn at little accuracy cost also dispensable for later turns when the history keeps the thinking, or do later turns depend on what within-turn importance scores discard?

- Lenses: composition; tension type: Measured in isolation: eviction scored within one turn, while multi-turn reuse changes what the cache is for
- Why unknown: ThinKV and R-KV score eviction within one turn. SCBench uses non-reasoning models and long shared inputs. CommitKV does not separate thinking tokens from other history and does not compare thinking kept versus stripped. No source measures whether the thinking tokens that are redundant for finishing the current answer are also redundant for a later turn that builds on that reasoning.
- If yes: Preserved thinking can be held at a small budget, which removes the memory objection to keeping thinking in history. This is a confirmation rather than a surprise, and with the default template (thinking stripped) on a dense model the stake is small, since only the last answer is recomputed. The stake is in agents that preserve thinking and in hybrid models.
- If no: Within-turn importance is the wrong signal for retention across turns. That points to scoring thinking tokens by later-turn use, and it qualifies the batch-size gains that within-turn eviction papers report for agent workloads.
- First experiment: Qwen3.6-35B-A3B in FP8 on one 48 GB card with preserve_thinking on (HuggingFace implementation, since R-KV is not in vLLM). Two-turn tasks built from MATH-500 and AIME where turn 2 asks about or builds on a step of turn 1, plus SCBench multi-turn subsets. Conditions for the turn-1 thinking: full KV; evicted to two budgets with R-KV scoring; stripped from history. Numbers: turn-2 accuracy, turn-2 reasoning tokens, and KV bytes held between turns.
- Who cares: Authors of KV eviction for reasoning models; teams serving agents with preserved thinking; engine maintainers deciding what to keep in the prefix cache between turns.
- Closest existing answer: CommitKV (preprint, SGLang) covers agent history as a whole against R-KV. SCBench (ICLR 2025) shows follow-up failures on non-reasoning models. KVzip (NeurIPS 2025) offers query-agnostic compression for reuse. None isolates thinking tokens or compares preserved versus stripped thinking.
- Overlap with dropped directions: D1 (KV selection for reasoning outputs) and D2 (prefix caching and reuse across turns)
- Evidence:
  - ThinKV, the reasoning-model eviction method with a paged-attention kernel, limits itself to output-dominated settings and has no multi-turn or prefix-caching evaluation. (ThinKV: Thought-Adaptive KV Cache Compression for Efficient Reasoning Models (ICLR 2026); https://arxiv.org/html/2510.01290): "it is not directly applicable to settings dominated by long input contexts."
  - On non-reasoning models with long shared contexts, eviction that works for the first query fails on follow-ups. (SCBench: A KV Cache-Centric Analysis of Long-Context Methods (ICLR 2025); https://arxiv.org/html/2412.10319): "This explains why O(k) KV cache compression methods perform well in single-query tests but fail in follow-ups."
  - A 2026 preprint on agent KV lifecycle states that current-turn attention does not predict later use and beats R-KV, but it treats history as a whole and does not separate thinking from other history. (CommitKV (arXiv preprint 2608.07855, 2026); https://arxiv.org/html/2608.07855): "low attention in the current turn does not imply future irrelevance"; at budget 2048 on Qwen3-14B it "outperforms the best R-KV method by 22.24%"
  - A current open model is trained to keep thinking in history and its card claims a KV-cache benefit, with no measurements. (Qwen3.6-35B-A3B model card (Hugging Face, 2026); https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.6-35B-A3B): "Qwen3.6 has been additionally trained to preserve and leverage thinking traces from historical messages."; "Additionally, it can improve KV cache utilization, optimizing inference efficiency in both thinking and non-thinking modes."
- Judges: surprise: SCBench and CommitKV already indicate that within-turn importance does not predict later use, so 'no' is expected and 'yes' has small stakes. / feasible: It needs R-KV ported to a 35B MoE hybrid in slow HF Transformers (FP8 loading is awkward, bf16 needs two cards) plus a custom-built two-turn dataset that reviewers may question. / generative: It gives one finding about cross-turn reuse of thinking KV; the design it would motivate (scoring by later-turn use) is largely taken by CommitKV, and it sits on D1 and D2. / reviewer: CommitKV already states and exploits that current-turn attention does not predict later-turn use and SCBench shows follow-up failures, so isolating thinking tokens is an incremental slice.

## 33. Q08 (mean 2.5, min 1; S2 F5 G2 R1)

With 30 questions x 64 samples the binomial standard error per arm is at most about 1.1 points (my arithmetic, treating samples as independent, which overstates precision because samples cluster by question); a 5% relative gain is of that order. No paper found tests the improvement on a set large enough to resolve it.

- If no: If noise: this is the less interesting answer, a negative replication. Its use is as a measured noise floor that 'lossless at X% budget' claims, and the small margins in D1, must clear.

Full cache versus R-KV at 10%, 16% and 34% budgets on MATH-500 (16 samples per question) and AIME 2024 + 2025 (64 samples). Run the full-cache arm twice at different batch sizes to measure the engine's own noise. Measured number: paired bootstrap confidence interval over questions for the accuracy difference, compared with the full-versus-full difference.
- Who cares: Authors and reviewers of KV-compression and quantization papers evaluated on AIME.
- Closest existing answer: "Hold Onto That Thought" (arXiv 2512.12008) re-benchmarks R-KV on more tasks and does not report above-baseline accuracy as a finding; no paper found that tests the claim with intervals.

- Evidence:
  - R-KV claims compression improves accuracy over the full cache on AIME-24. (R-KV: Redundancy-aware KV Cache Compression for Reasoning Models, NeurIPS 2025; https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.24133): Remarkably, R-KV even achieves 105% accuracy of the full KV baseline with around 16% of the KV cache using DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B on the AIME-24 dataset. ## 34. Q15 (mean 2.5, min 1; S3 F4 G1 R2)

**Question.** Is the prefix-cache cliff reported for concurrent agents (hit rate from about 98% to 17-27%) caused by preemption and readmission of running requests, rather than by eviction of idle sessions' prefixes during tool waits?

- Lenses: anomalies; tension type: An unexplained step change in a measurement paper; a systems paper that asserts one mechanism without counting it; the student's logs show a second mechanism that produces the same symptom and is removed by one engine flag.
- Why unknown: Neither paper counts how many lost cache hits come from each mechanism. The IISWC table is hard to read under the idle-eviction account alone, because prefill stays under 10% of request time while throughput falls 44-66%; part of that fall is ordinary batching at higher concurrency, and the paper does not separate the two. The student's measurement has no tool waits, so it shows the second mechanism exists but not how large it is for agents. Whether Qwen3.6-27B is a hybrid is not stated in the IISWC paper and was not checked.
- If yes: Part of the published cliff, and part of the baseline gap that program-aware schedulers report, comes from an engine default. The first thing to report would be how much of the cliff one flag removes.
- If no: Idle-prefix eviction dominates, which confirms the premise of ThunderAgent and Continuum and shows the watermark does not matter for agent workloads. This is the less new answer.
- First experiment: One hybrid and one dense model of about 27-30B on vLLM; replay agent trajectories with recorded tool-wait times (the IISWC artifact, or mini-SWE-agent runs); sweep concurrency across the cliff with watermark 0 and 0.1; use the existing scheduler instrumentation (scripts/profile thrash_trace.py) to tag each recomputed prompt token by cause (blocks freed by preempting a running request vs blocks of an idle session evicted). The number: the share of recomputed tokens from each cause, and the hit rate at the cliff concurrency with watermark 0.1.
- Who cares: Authors of agent-serving schedulers; vLLM maintainers; people who run agent fleets or RL rollouts on a fixed GPU budget.
- Closest existing answer: ThunderAgent and Continuum (arXiv 2511.02230) assume idle eviction and build for it. The student's R1 result covers batch rollouts without tool waits. No paper found that separates the two causes.
- Overlap with dropped directions: Heavy. D4: the watermark result is the D4 finding, and D4 was dropped because the watermark alone came within 7% of a perfect schedule; the student's evidence already leans towards yes in the setting without tool waits. D2: it concerns prefix caching. D3: it reuses the profiling tools.
- Evidence:
  - The measurement paper reports the cliff and its throughput cost but gives no mechanism beyond 'evicts useful cached prefixes', and no engine flags beyond the vLLM version and TP=2. Prefill stays under 10% of request time while per-request throughput falls 44-66%. (Agentic AI Workload Characterization, IISWC 2026 (venue inferred from the paper's artifact appendix), arXiv 2605.26297; https://arxiv.org/html/2605.26297): Table II, Qwen3.6-27B. Terminal-Bench 2.0 at 15 / 20 / 25 jobs: prefix-cache hit 98.85 / 16.78 / 19.21%; generation throughput 30.08 / 16.96 / 10.30 tok/s per request; prefill 4.42 / 8.38 / 7.81% of request time. The default cost the hybrid 26-37% and the dense model 5-7.6% against a perfect-packing bound.
- Judges: surprise: A 'yes' would show part of a published cliff and of scheduler baseline gaps is an engine default, which is notable, but 'no' only confirms the existing premise and the student's data already leans yes. / feasible: The student already has the per-cause recompute instrumentation and the watermark flag, and 27-30B FP8 models fit at TP=2, but the cliff will sit at a much lower concurrency than on 2x H100 and the trajectories must be replayed from an artifact or generated. / generative: It attributes a published cliff to an engine default (the watermark) that the student has already raised upstream, which makes it a version-specific detail and a D4 repeat. / reviewer: Attributing the cache cliff to preemption versus idle eviction is a sharp but narrow engine-default diagnosis that essentially re-reports the dropped D4 watermark finding on a different workload.
